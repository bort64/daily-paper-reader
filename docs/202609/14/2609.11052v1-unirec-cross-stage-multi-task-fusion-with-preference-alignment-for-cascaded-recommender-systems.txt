## Abstract 

Industrial recommender systems use cascaded stages with different objectives, feature spaces, and latency constraints. Optimizing pre-ranking and ranking separately can create cross-stage inconsistency. Upstream models may filter out items preferred by downstream rankers, while independently tuned downstream fusion can off-set improvements in upstream fusion. Existing multi-task fusion methods mainly focus on multi-objective fusion within the ranking stage. Cross-stage methods typically use downstream signals to add a score factor to upstream ranking. Joint optimization of fusion modules across both stages remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, we propose UniRec, a Uni fied Cross-stage 

Rec ommendation Fusion model. First, the two fusion agents par-tially share their input embeddings and are trained within a sin-gle computation graph, allowing gradients from either stage to propagate through the shared representation and influence the other stage. Second, we introduce a dual-axis preference alignment objective to coordinate the two stages. Along the vertical axis, a cross-stage consistency term transfers downstream pairwise pref-erences to the upstream fusion score. Along the horizontal axis, a compact aggregation term reorganizes dozens of pairwise objec-tives over heterogeneous prior signals into bidirectional preference evidence. Third, we find that unconstrained end-to-end fusion opti-mization can exploit imbalances in the item attribute distribution, over-concentrating on high-reward regions while sacrificing other important recommendation objectives. ## 1 Introduction 

Industrial recommender systems are commonly deployed as cas-caded pipelines consisting of matching, pre-ranking, ranking, and re-ranking, in order to balance recommendation quality with strict online latency constraints [18]. As illustrated in Figure 1, the pre-ranking stage filters thousands of candidates under a tight latency budget, while the downstream ranking stage performs computation-ally intensive multi-objective optimization to produce the final list. However, independent optimization induces cross-stage prefer-ence inconsistency. Pre-ranking fixes the candidate pool, so items favored by ranking may be discarded irreversibly, while ranking is optimized over a distribution it cannot correct. Pipeline quality is thus bounded by its weakest stage rather than the global optimum. Existing remedies rely on cross-stage distillation or cascade co-ordination: COPR [26], unbiased pre-ranking [24], and hybrid co-ordination [25] transfer ranking supervision to upstream stages to reduce selection bias. However, these methods only refine pre-ranking scores while leaving the fusion process untouched. As shown in Figure 1, the final pre-ranking decision is produced by a multi-task fusion module, whose ensemble formulation is often manually designed. Even with better-aligned pre-ranking scores, the resulting fused score may still differ substantially from down-stream preferences. Multi-task fusion is also a core optimization component in both pre-ranking and ranking, and has been studied using hand-designed strategies, heuristic search [9, 11], reinforce-ment learning [3, 22, 23], and end-to-end learning [5, 20]. However, these methods predominantly target the ranking stage, leaving pre-ranking and ranking fusion modules independently optimized and the cross-stage inconsistency unresolved. Jointly optimizing the two fusion modules, however, introduces two challenges. First, industrial recommender systems involve dozens of heterogeneous objectives, making naive aggregation of their pairwise preference losses increasingly costly and difficult to scale. Second, end-to-end optimization can amplify attribute-dependent reward bias [21], causing the model to over-optimize high-reward attribute groups at the expense of other objectives. These challenges motivate a unified framework that jointly opti-mizes fusion across stages while controlling such optimization bias. We therefore argue that fusion modules, rather than only scoring models, should be optimized jointly across cascaded stages. We propose UniRec, a Uni fied Cross-stage Rec ommendation Fusion model, which optimizes both stages’ fusion modules under downstream-aware supervision and reformulates stage-wise opti-mization as end-to-end cross-stage preference optimization. UniRec consists of three complementary components. First, we develop a jointly trained cross-stage architecture. The two fusion agents par-tially share their input embeddings, allowing gradients to flow in both directions. An adapter network further injects the pre-ranking representation into the ranking agent as an explicit feature. To our knowledge, this is the first framework to optimize the fusion mod-ules of two cascaded stages as a single differentiable module in the industrial recommender system. Second, we introduce a dual-axis preference alignment objective. Along the vertical axis, a cross-stage consistency term transfers downstream pairwise preferences to the upstream fusion score. Along the horizontal axis, a compact aggregation term reorganizes dozens of heterogeneous pairwise ob-jectives into two bidirectional preference signals. Third, we propose an Attribute Group-Relative Regularization(AGRR) method to con-trol attribute-dependent optimization bias. Inspired by the grouping principle of GRPO [13], it replaces sampled rollouts with candidates sharing the same attribute group and computes both advantages and policy normalization within each group. As a result, uniformly promoting a high-reward attribute group provides no additional optimization gain, preventing the end-to-end objective from ex-ploiting systematic attribute-level reward differences. Offline and large-scale online experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of UniRec, which is fully deployed in production. Our contributions are as follows: 

• We formulate end-to-end cross-stage multi-task fusion as a new optimization paradigm for cascaded recommenders, realized by a jointly trained architecture that enables cross-stage gradient flow and mutual adaptation. 

• We design two complementary objectives for this architec-ture. The dual-axis preference alignment objective super-vises the fusion space both across stages and across business objectives, while the attribute group-relative regularization prevents end-to-end optimization from exploiting systematic attribute-dependent reward differences. 

• We conduct extensive offline and large-scale online A/B ex-periments on a production short-video system, demonstrat-ing consistent improvements over cross-stage coordination and single-stage fusion baselines. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Pre-ranking optimization and cross-stage coordination 

Recent work improves pre-ranking through better models and co-ordination with downstream ranking. COLD [18] is a cost-aware pre-ranking system that admits arbitrarily deep models under a latency budget. Others target cross-stage consistency: COPR [26] aligns pre-ranking scores with the ranking model via chunk-based sampling and a ΔNDCG-weighted loss; Rethink [24] proposes an All-Scenario hit-rate (ASH) metric and a multi-objective loss (AS-MOL) optimizing the pre-ranking set rather than its ordering; HCCP [25] couples upstream retrieval signals with downstream feedback through hybrid sampling over unexposed candidates and a margin-based InfoNCE loss [10]. Together they show that transferring rank-ing supervision upstream mitigates selection bias and improves upstream representations. This line of work shares two properties. First, supervision flows one way: the ranking model acts as a frozen teacher and receives no gradient from the pre-ranking objective, so the two stages remain separate problems coupled by a signal rather than a single joint one. Second, they optimize only the pre-ranking scoring model and leave fusion unchanged. As shown in Figure 1, industrial systems fuse multiple objectives (e.g. click, watch time, like) via fixed formulas or independently trained weights, and this fusion module produces the final pre-ranking decision. Even with better-aligned scores, the fused output may still diverge from ranking preferences, leaving the module that actually determines candidate selection outside cross-stage optimization. 

## 2.2 Multi-task fusion and end-to-end ranking 

Industrial recommenders formulate both pre-ranking and ranking as multi-task problems, predicting multiple engagement objectives (e.g. click-through rate, watch time, like) and aggregating them through a fusion module into a unified score for candidate selection, which makes fusion design a fundamental problem. Early systems used hand-designed formulas with coefficients later searched by heuristics such as the Cross-Entropy Method [11] and Bayesian Op-timization [9]. Subsequent work casts fusion as sequential decision-making optimized by reinforcement learning: BatchRL-MTF [23] learns fusion weights via offline RL, RLUR [1] optimizes retention-oriented policies, and xMTF [3] introduces a learnable monotonic fusion cell. UNEX-RL [22] exploits the unidirectional dependency of cascaded systems through a multi-agent information chain, but its agents output weight vectors of fixed formulas rather than learn-ing the fusion models, and their parameters receive no cross-stage gradient. End-to-end ensemble ranking instead learns the fusion model itself: EMER [5] adopts a transformer architecture, UMRE [20] learns unconstrained monotonic fusion functions under Pareto-optimal multi-objective optimization [6, 12], Pantheon [2] performs personalized ensemble ranking via iterative Pareto policy optimiza-tion, and HarmonRank [19] aligns multi-objective fusion with rank-ing metrics through attention. Orthogonally, a line addresses attribute-induced bias in short-video recommendation, where feedback is confounded by attributes such as video duration [21]. These methods debias individual task signals, whereas we leave cross-attribute preference to the fusion ob-jective and restrict the group-relative advantage to within-attribute comparisons, so that uniformly promoting a high-reward region is no longer a valid optimization direction. Despite this progress, prior work either aligns the pre-ranking scoring model through downstream supervision or improves fusion within a single stage. Jointly optimizing the fusion modules across cascaded stages re-mains unstudied, which is the problem we address.

## 4 Method 

Figure 2 illustrates the overall architecture of UniRec. Instead of op-timizing the pre-ranking and ranking stages independently, UniRec places a fusion agent at each stage and couples the two within a sin-gle differentiable graph, so that both fusion modules are optimized against a shared objective. The framework takes only the outputs of the stage-specific multi-task prediction models (e.g., pXTRs) as inputs and remains decoupled from their underlying backbones, enabling UniRec to be optimized independently on top of existing prediction models.

4.1.2 Stage-specific fusion towers. Each agent applies its own Deep & Cross Network (DCN) [17] to model explicit feature crosses, fol-lowed by a fusion tower chosen according to its latency budget. ## 4.3 Attribute Group-Relative Regularization 

The pairwise objectives of Sec. 4.2 admit a degenerate solution: because attributes correlate with preference evidence, an agent can reduce a large share of the loss by shifting scores toward the attribute region that tends to win. Long videos accumulate more positive watch-time evidence, so promoting them as a group is rewarded even when intra-range ordering does not improve; online this appears as drift of the exposed duration distribution rather than as a gain in user satisfaction. Application to both agents. The regularizer is applied to both agents, but the reward is constructed once, at the ranking stage: for the pre-ranking agent the rewards of Eq. 11 are transferred along the candidate correspondence between the two stages, candidates without a downstream counterpart are excluded, and grouping fol-lows the upstream agent’s own buckets. This anchors the upstream regularizer to the downstream notion of item quality, constraining both agents against a common reward scale. Cross-attribute preferences are still preserved: the pairwise terms of Sec. 4.2 run over all candidate pairs, most of which span different buckets, and remain responsible for ordering items of different dura-tions. Eq. 14 restricts only the source of the advantage, withdrawing the incentive to inflate an attribute region while preserving the ability to rank across regions; it is given a small weight, acting as a stabilizer of the attribute distribution rather than as a driver of the ranking. 

## 4.4 Joint Optimization 

Collecting the terms above, each agent is optimized against its own preference supervision together with the AGRR, and the two are trained under a single objective:

(15) The alignment term appears only on the pre-ranking side, since the preference to be aligned is by definition the downstream one. The gradient coupling, however, is symmetric: both objectives update 

𝜃 sh in a single backward pass, so each reshapes the representation on which the other’s score is computed. The adapter of Sec. 4.1 adds a further upstream-to-downstream path, carrying the pre-ranking representation into 𝑠 𝑟 in the forward pass while remaining detached in the backward pass. This is what distinguishes Eq. (15) from the two independent problems of Eq. (3), in which neither stage is aware of the other. At serving time the two agents are exported as separate graphs, since they run at different points of the cascade, and the shared embedding table is replicated. Joint optimization therefore adds no serving latency: the coupling exists only during training, and each agent is served exactly as a stage-local fusion model. 

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate UniRec through four research questions: 

• RQ1: Does UniRec outperform single-stage fusion and cross-stage coordination methods in ranking quality and cross-stage consistency? 

• RQ2: How much does each component contribute? 

• RQ3: How sensitive is UniRec to the loss weights? 

• RQ4: Does the AGRR improve cross-stage consistency with-out reducing ranking quality? 

## 5.1 Datasets and Experimental Settings 

5.1.1 RecFlow: Public Cascaded Benchmark. RecFlow 1 is collected from Kuaishou’s multi-stage short-video recommendation system. It contains 37 days of logs from January 13 to February 18, 2024. The dataset includes about 42K users, 9.3M requests, 38M exposure samples, and 1.9B stage-level records. The recommendation system has six stages, with candidate sizes of 8000 → 3000 → 500 →

100 → 10 → 6. RecFlow records the candidates removed at each stage. We use these records to reconstruct the nested candidate sets 

I𝑐 ⊃ I 𝑝 ⊃ I 𝑟 in Eq. 2. They also provide the shared candidate set I𝑝 required by L𝑎𝑙𝑖𝑔𝑛 in Eq. 7. We use eight days of exposure logs from February 1 to February 8. Each sample contains cross-stage candidate information and a 50-step behavior sequence. Each item has six binary labels and a playing_time label. Following [4], we process playing_time with weighted logistic regression. This produces 𝑀 =7 inputs for the fusion model, with positive rates ranging from 0.2% to 38.2%. 

5.1.2 Industrial Production Dataset. We also evaluate UniRec on data from Kuaishou’s online recommendation system, which has more than 100M daily active users. For each request, the pre-ranking stage selects candidates from a set of about 500 items. The follow-ing stages reduce this set to about 10 exposed items. For offline evaluation, we sample about 100K users and obtain more than 1M request-level samples. The fusion model handles dozens of prediction objectives. In this setting, the O( 𝑀𝑁 2) cost of Eq. 6 is expensive, making the dataset suitable for evaluating CPPA. 

5.1.3 Experimental Settings. RecFlow does not provide the pXTRs of pre-ranking and ranking models. We therefore train two 𝑀 =7-head backbone models to generate pXTRs. Following the different capacity requirements of the two stages, we use DSSM for 𝑓 𝑝 and DIN for 𝑓 𝑟 . We train the backbones on data from February 1 to February 4 and then freeze their parameters. The frozen models generate prediction scores for each stage. We train the fusion mod-els on data from February 5 to February 7 and evaluate them on February 8. All methods use the same cached prediction scores to ensure a fair comparison. For the industrial dataset, we split the offline logs in chronological order.

## 5.3 Offline Evaluation 

Following RecFlow [7], we evaluate candidates per request. We compute NDCG@ 𝐾 for 𝑠 𝑟 𝑖 and 𝑠 𝑝 𝑖 , where 𝐾 ∈ { 10 , 30 , 50 } and 𝑟 𝑖 

is the graded relevance label. For rank_AUC, we pool candidates across requests and use ⊮[𝑟 𝑖 > 0] as the binary label. We use ASH [24] as the main metric for cross-stage consistency. It measures the fraction of the ranking-stage Top-𝐾 𝑟 items retained by pre-ranking, where 𝐾 𝑟 =10 and 𝐾 𝑝 =50. We also report Kendall’s 𝜏 and Spearman’s 𝜌 . They measure the rank correlation between 𝑠 𝑝 𝑖 and 

𝑠 𝑟 𝑖 within each request. 

Ranking quality. Table 1 reports the results on RecFlow. UniRec achieves the best NDCG at all six cutoffs. UMRE has a slightly higher rank_AUC, but its NDCG is lower at every cutoff. The two metrics evaluate different aspects of performance. rank_AUC is computed across requests, whereas NDCG evaluates the ranking within each request. Since candidate selection is performed within each request, NDCG is more relevant to this setting. 

Cross-stage consistency. As shown in Table 1, UniRec achieves the best results on all three consistency metrics. Its ASH is 0.9978, higher than Weighted-Sum(0.9648), EMER (0.7219), UMRE (0.9855), and COPR (0.9668). UniRec also obtains the highest Kendall’s 𝜏 and Spearman’s 𝜌 , with values of 0.6318 and 0.8072, respectively. EMER has the lowest values among all methods. These results show that independent stage-wise optimization can lead to weak cross-stage consistency and may cause the pre-ranking stage to remove items preferred by the ranking stage. 

## 5.4 Ablation Study 

We answer RQ2 using Kuaishou’s industrial production dataset. Each variant removes one loss term from Eq. 15, while all other settings remain unchanged. 

The loss terms affect different metrics. As shown in Fig. 3, the full model achieves the best overall performance. Removing the posterior reward loss causes the largest decrease across all six metrics. This loss provides the main signal for balancing the prediction objectives. Removing the prior loss causes the second-largest decrease in ranking quality, but has the smallest effect on ASH. The prior loss is applied to both stages, so removing it affects their scores in a similar way. AGRR mainly improves cross-stage consistency. Except for reward removal, removing AGRR causes the largest decrease in ASH (-0.0215), while the ranking metrics change only slightly. AGRR reduces attribute-related bias and helps the two stages produce more consistent rankings. 

Alignment improves only the upstream stage. As shown in Figure 3, removing the alignment loss produces the same results as the full model across the three ranking metrics. This follows from the design of L𝑎𝑙𝑖𝑔𝑛 . The ranking score is used as a teacher signal with stopped gradients, so L𝑎𝑙𝑖𝑔𝑛 does not directly update the ranking-specific parameters. Its main effect is therefore observed in the pre-ranking stage and cross-stage consistency. Removing it re-duces ASH by 0.0139. This result supports the claim in Section 4.2.1 that downstream preferences can be transferred to the upstream stage without reducing downstream ranking quality. 

w/o CPPA. This variant replaces CPPA with the standard formu-lation computed separately for each objective. The two formulations are mathematically equivalent, so they achieve the same ranking performance. However, CPPA is more efficient. It increases training throughput by 30.1% and reduces the processing time per sample by 23.1%.

No reported metric decreases. This indicates that the improvements are not achieved by sacrificing one objective for another. UniRec has now been fully deployed.

address this problem, we propose UniRec, a unified framework that jointly optimizes the pre-ranking and ranking fusion agents. UniRec connects the two agents through shared input embeddings and a one-way adapter. It transfers downstream pairwise prefer-ences to the pre-ranking stage through cross-stage alignment. It also introduces CPPA, which groups multiple task-specific prefer-ences into two aggregated signals without changing the original objective. This reduces the number of differentiable terms from 

O(( 𝑀𝑁 2) to O( 𝑁 2). In addition, the AGRR loss reduces reward bias associated with attributes such as video duration. Experiments on RecFlow and Kuaishou’s production data show that UniRec improves both ranking quality and cross-stage consistency. It out-performs stage-wise optimization, cross-stage coordination meth-ods, and strong single-stage fusion models. In a one-week online A/B test, UniRec improves total watch time by +0.675% and app usage time by +0.616%, with no decrease in the reported metrics. UniRec has been fully deployed. In future work, we plan to extend UniRec to the re-ranking stage, other bias-related attributes, and recommendation scenarios such as e-commerce and advertising.
