Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can infer user preferences from inter-
action histories and reviews, yet the rationales they generate may
not reflect the information actually used for recommendation. A
preference claim may be weakly supported by its selected evidence,
or may have little effect on the final ranking. We refer to these two
failures as the grounding–influence gap. We introduce PROVE-Rec,
a general framework for verifiable preference reasoning in LLM-
based recommendation. Pass A converts the complete pre-target
history into a compact preference proof consisting of positive and
avoidance claims linked to selected evidence entries. Pass B predicts
the next item using only the proof and its selected evidence, prevent-
ing the recommender from bypassing the reasoning path. To verify
evidence-to-proof grounding, we compare the effect of masking se-
lected evidence with masking a comparable control entry. To verify
proof-to-recommendation influence, we remove a preference claim
and measure the resulting decrease in the target item’s ranking
margin. A ranking-preservation objective further retains useful in-
formation from the complete history. Comprehensive experiments
on wide-ranging real-world datasets demonstrate that PROVE-Rec
consistently outperforms strong sequential, generative, and LLM-
enhanced baselines, with improvements of up to 7.45%. Controlled
ablations confirm the effectiveness of the two-pass architecture
and verification objectives. Moreover, PROVE-Rec produces claims
that are more strongly grounded in historical evidence and more
influential to recommendation while preserving ranking quality.

Plausibility, however, leaves two questions unanswered. First,
does E1 actually support the inferred preference for comfort, or
is the evidence link merely decorative? Second, does the comfort
claim affect the target ranking, or can the recommender ignore the
proof and rely on category transitions, item popularity, or other
shortcuts? As illustrated in Figure 1, the former failure occurs when
masking E1 barely changes the likelihood of the comfort claim,

whereas the latter occurs when changing comfort from a positive
preference to an avoidance preference leaves the target item’s rank-

menders primarily optimize recommendation accuracy, rationale
likelihood, or trajectory-level reward [10, 18, 32]. These objectives
do not verify whether the stated evidence supports the preference
or whether the preference affects the recommendation. Other LLM
recommenders incorporate collaborative signals or reviews to im-
prove recommendation quality [30, 31], while rationale-faithfulness
research examines whether explanations reflect the evidence be-
hind model predictions [5]. However, these directions do not jointly
verify both dependencies in LLM-based recommendation.
We use the term grounding–influence gap to describe two
distinct operational dependencies. Evidence-to-proof grounding asks
whether the generation of a preference claim is selectively depen-
dent on the historical evidence linked to that claim, relative to a
comparable control entry. Proof-to-recommendation influence asks
whether the current ranking is locally sensitive to the claim when
the selected evidence is held fixed. These two properties provide
complementary views of the model’s reasoning behavior, but they
do not define preference correctness. A semantically valid prefer-
ence may be redundant with other claims or irrelevant to a particu-
lar target and therefore exhibit little measurable influence in that
recommendation.
Our method is built on two complementary ideas.
(Idea 1): make evidence-to-proof grounding explicit. Instead of
producing unrestricted chain-of-thought text, the LLM generates a
structured preference proof : a compact set of positive or avoidance
claims, each linked to one or more evidence identifiers. We use
the term proof operationally rather than logically; it denotes a
checkable claim–evidence structure rather than a formal derivation.
This structure exposes the evidence-to-proof link to a controlled
dependence test. For each claim, we mask its selected evidence and
compare the resulting decrease in claim likelihood with that caused
by masking a comparable control entry. A grounded claim should
depend more strongly on its selected evidence than on the control
entry.
(Idea 2): make proof-to-recommendation influence explicit.
The subsequent recommendation pass receives only the prefer-
ence proof and the history entries selected by it. This proof-gated
path prevents the model from generating a rationale while still
predicting directly from the complete history. We then test the
proof-to-recommendation link by removing one preference claim
while keeping the selected evidence and the remaining proof un-
changed. If the claim is functionally used, its removal should reduce
the target item’s ranking margin. A format control changes only
the presentation of the proof, separating semantic influence from
generic prompt sensitivity. Finally, a listwise preservation objective
keeps the proof-gated ranking distribution close to that obtained
from the complete history.
The framework is not tied to a particular item representation.
It applies to any autoregressive LLM recommender that can score
the token sequence used to identify or describe an item. In our
experiments, we instantiate PROVE-Rec on a strong generative
recommender [23, 31]; this choice defines the experimental imple-
mentation rather than the scope of the framework.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify the grounding–influence gap in LLM-based rec-
ommendation, where a generated preference rationale may
be weakly supported by its selected evidence or have little
effect on the final recommendation.
• We introduce a compact preference proof and a proof-gated
recommendation architecture that explicitly links prefer-
ence claims to historical evidence and prevents the recom-
mender from bypassing the generated proof.
• We develop evidence-masking, claim-ablation, and ranking-
preservation objectives to verify the evidence-to-proof and
proof-to-recommendation links while maintaining recom-
mendation utility.
• We provide theoretical results that bound grounding and
influence failures and characterize when the proof-gated
model preserves the top-𝐾ranking of the full-history rec-
ommender.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 3 in-
troduces the recommendation setting, evidence history, and preference-
proof formulation. Section 4 presents PROVE-Rec, including preference-
proof induction, proof-gated recommendation, evidence-to-proof
grounding, proof-to-recommendation influence, and ranking preser-

evidence–proof–recommendation path. Finally, we summarize the
main findings and conclude the paper.
2
Related Work
2.1
LLM-Based Recommendation
Early work such as P5 reformulated multiple recommendation
tasks as language-processing problems, enabling a shared sequence-
to-sequence model to consume interaction histories, metadata,
and natural-language prompts [8]. Subsequent methods adapted
general-purpose LLMs to recommendation through parameter-
efficient tuning, collaborative-semantic alignment, or explicit in-
terfaces between behavioral representations and language mod-
els [2, 17, 29, 31]. More recently, RALLRec+ combines retrieval-
augmented recommendation with LLM reasoning, further demon-
strating the potential of integrating retrieved behavioral evidence
with language-model inference [19]. These studies show that lan-
guage knowledge and collaborative signals can complement each
other for recommendation. However, they primarily focus on rec-
ommendation quality and model adaptation rather than directly
examining whether an explicit preference claim selectively depends
on particular historical evidence or contributes to the resulting
ranking.
2.2
Generative Recommendation and Item
Tokenization
Generative recommenders identify items through autoregressive to-
ken sequences instead of scoring every item independently. TIGER
introduced semantic identifiers for generative retrieval, while LET-
TER learned item codes that better combine semantic and collabo-
rative information [23, 25]. LC-Rec further integrates collaborative
semantics into LLM adaptation, and DIGER studies differentiable

semantic identifiers for end-to-end generative recommendation
[7, 31]. Beyond single-domain tokenization, UniTok develops a uni-
fied item-tokenization framework for multi-domain LLM-based
recommendation, combining shared and domain-specific experts
with codebook-based identifiers to preserve both transferable and
domain-specific item semantics [12]. These studies demonstrate the
importance of item representations in connecting the discrete item
space with autoregressive language models. In contrast, PROVE-
Rec leaves the underlying item representation unchanged and in-
stead modifies the information path through which historical evi-
dence and explicit preference claims contribute to item prediction.
2.3
Review-Aware and Reasoning-Enhanced
Recommendation
Reviews provide fine-grained signals about user preferences and
item characteristics that are often absent from item-only interac-
tion sequences. Prior review-aware recommenders have used deep
Transformer architectures to extract textual preference signals and
improve personalized recommendation [9]. AENAR further models
aspect-level information from reviews to capture diverse user pref-
erences and provide interpretable evidence for rating prediction
[28]. More recently, RAGR directly incorporates review represen-
tations into generative user sequences and aligns the resulting
representations with item prediction [30]. In contrast, PROVE-Rec
organizes historical reviews into a compact set of evidence-linked
preference claims and explicitly examines whether each claim de-
pends selectively on its cited evidence.
Recent reasoning-enhanced recommenders generate explicit or
latent reasoning before predicting an item. OneRec-Think intro-
duces in-text reasoning for generative recommendation, SIDRea-
soner reasons over semantic identifiers, R2Rank optimizes reason-
ing for ranking utility, and RE2 studies reasoning for more inter-
pretable recommendation predictions [4, 10, 18, 32]. RALLRec+
additionally combines retrieval augmentation with reasoning LLMs
to enhance recommendation [19]. These methods demonstrate that
intermediate reasoning can improve recommendation, but recom-
mendation accuracy or trajectory-level objectives alone do not
directly reveal how individual reasoning statements depend on
historical evidence or affect a particular ranking decision. PROVE-
Rec focuses on these two complementary dependencies through
evidence-to-proof grounding and proof-to-recommendation influ-
ence.
2.4
Rationale Grounding and
Intervention-Based Evaluation
A fluent rationale can appear plausible without revealing how it
relates to the evidence or the model’s final prediction. ERASER
evaluates rationales through evidence-based metrics and pertur-
bation tests [5]. Faithful Chain-of-Thought makes an intermediate
symbolic reasoning representation necessary for producing the
final answer, while later work examines whether generated reason-
ing traces have measurable effects on model predictions [20, 22].
Related work in recommender systems has also explored post-hoc
explainability through deletion-based diagnostics that measure how
individual user or item information affects recommendation behav-
ior [1]. These studies motivate intervention-based evaluation, but
they do not jointly address recommendation-specific questions
such as preference polarity, evidence selection from sequential
user histories, claim-level ranking effects, and preservation of rec-
ommendation utility. PROVE-Rec carries this intervention-based
perspective into LLM-based recommendation with two paired tests
on the evidence–proof–recommendation path: whether masking a
claim’s linked evidence lowers its score more than masking a com-
parable control entry, and whether removing the claim lowers the
target item’s ranking margin while the selected evidence remains
fixed.

5.1
Datasets, Preprocessing, and
Preference-Proof Construction
We evaluate PROVE-Rec on on two Amazon review domains [21],
Grocery and Tools, together with Yelp1 All datasets contain user–
item interactions together with textual reviews, enabling us to
construct evidence-enriched interaction histories and preference
proofs. Table 1 summarizes the processed datasets.
Data preprocessing. For each user, interactions are ordered chrono-
logically. The last interaction is used for testing, the second-to-last
for validation, and the remaining interactions for training. Each
model input contains only interactions that occur before the corre-
sponding target, and previously interacted items are excluded from
the recommendation candidates during evaluation.
Each example retains at most 20 pre-target interactions. Ratings
and reviews are included for the three most recent entries, with
review text truncated to 64 characters and review summaries to 32
characters. Reviews are aligned with the processed interaction se-
quences using user and item identifiers, while ambiguous duplicate
matches are excluded. The target item, its rating and review, and
all future interactions are removed from both Pass A and Pass B to
prevent temporal leakage.
Preference-proof construction. Pass A is supervised using offline
preference proofs constructed exclusively from the pre-target his-
tory. Each proof consists of at most 𝐾𝐶preference claims and selects
at most 𝐾𝐸historical evidence entries. Each claim contains a nor-
malized preference aspect, a polarity (prefer or avoid), and one or
more identifiers of supporting history entries.
Candidate proofs are retained only if they satisfy the required
output format, contain valid and unique evidence identifiers, respect
the claim and evidence budgets, and contain no target or future
information. We additionally check that every cited identifier refers
to an entry in the corresponding pre-target history. These filters en-
sure structural validity, referential consistency, budget compliance,
and explicit leakage prevention; they are not treated as tests of
semantic entailment between a claim and its cited evidence. Table 2
reports the resulting proof statistics.

Experimental Setup
Implementation Details. Our implementation uses a semantic-
code generative recommender built on TIGER and LC-Rec [23, 31].
For each example, we retain up to 20 historical evidence entries and
include ratings and reviews for the three most recent entries. Review
text and item metadata are truncated to fixed lengths. We preserve
the original item tokenizer, recommendation objective, and catalog-
constrained decoding procedure. Additional implementation and
hyperparameter details are provided in the supplementary material.
Baselines. We compare PROVE-Rec with three groups of rep-
resentative methods: 1) Traditional and transformer-based recom-
menders include SASRec [14] and BERT4Rec [24]; 2) Item-tokenization-
based LLM recommenders include P5-SemID [8], TIGER [23], LC-
Rec [31], and DIGER [7]; 3) LLM-enhanced recommenders include
CoLLM [29], LLM-SRec [16], SIDReasoner [11], and RE2 [4].
For controlled analysis, we additionally construct same-backbone
variants that remove or replace individual components of PROVE-
Rec, including direct recommendation, free-form rationale genera-
tion, structured preference proofs without verification, and variants
without grounding, influence, or ranking preservation.
Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate recommendation performance
using Recall@𝑀(R@𝑀) and NDCG@𝑀(N@𝑀), where 𝑀∈{5, 10}.
For each user, previously interacted items are excluded from the
candidate set. All generative methods use catalog-constrained de-
coding to ensure that generated item codes correspond to valid
catalog items [23, 31].

5.4
RQ2: Are Preference Claims Grounded and
Influential?
We conduct an ablation study to assess the contribution of each
major component. Direct Recommendation removes the two-pass
architecture and predicts the next item directly from the complete
history. The remaining variants remove evidence-to-proof ground-
ing, proof-to-recommendation influence, or ranking preservation,
respectively. All variants use the same backbone, item representa-
tion, training data, and evaluation settings.
As shown in Table 4, the full model achieves the best Recall@10
and NDCG@10 on all three datasets. Direct Recommendation per-
forms worst, with an average relative decrease of 12.0%. This con-
firms the benefit of first extracting a compact preference proof and
then recommending from the proof and its selected evidence. Re-
moving evidence-to-proof grounding and proof-to-recommendation
influence leads to average decreases of 6.1% and 4.3%, respectively.
Thus, encouraging preference claims to depend on their selected ev-
idence and to affect the final recommendation also improves target-
item ranking. Removing ranking preservation causes a smaller but
consistent decrease of 2.4%, showing that it helps retain useful
information from the complete history. Overall, the two-pass archi-
tecture provides the largest gain, while grounding, influence, and
preservation offer complementary improvements.

5.5
RQ3: Which Information Sources and
Reasoning Designs Matter?
Table 5 compares different input signals and reasoning designs un-
der the same backbone and evaluation settings. Ratings consistently
improve over item-only histories by providing preference polarity,
while reviews yield larger gains by revealing fine-grained aspects
of user feedback. Combining ratings and reviews performs best
among the input-only variants, confirming that the two signals are
complementary.
For reasoning design, Direct Review Input predicts directly from
the review-enriched history, while Free-Form Rationale generates
an unconstrained natural-language rationale before recommenda-
tion. Structured Preference Proof instead represents reasoning as
compact claims linked to selected evidence. Free-form rationales
do not consistently outperform direct review input, suggesting that
unconstrained reasoning may introduce noise or be weakly used
by the recommender. Structured preference proofs perform better
across all datasets, showing the benefit of a concise and evidence-
linked intermediate representation. The full PROVE-Rec achieves
the strongest performance by further verifying the evidence-to-
proof and proof-to-recommendation links while preserving recom-
mendation utility.

Table 6 presents a representative example from Grocery to illus-

selection to preference-proof construction and next-item recom-
mendation. The user’s pre-target history contains multiple inter-
actions, from which PROVE-Rec selects three entries, E1, E3, and

E1 and E5 both describe positively rated soup or ramen products
and explicitly mention ease of preparation, supporting the prefer-
ences for savory Asian soup products and easy preparation. E3, a
positively rated herbal tea, provides complementary evidence for
the broader preference for healthy products with good taste. The
remaining interactions are not included in the proof because they
provide weaker support for these selected claims.
The resulting proof therefore summarizes several aspects of the
user’s history rather than relying on a single recent interaction.
These aspects are also consistent with the held-out target, instant
miso soup: its product type is related to the soup-oriented prefer-
ence supported by E1 and E5, while its convenience and health-
related characteristics are connected to the remaining claims. Using
the proof together with its selected evidence, PROVE-Rec ranks
the held-out target at 1st. This case does not by itself establish a
general performance advantage, but it illustrates how PROVE-Rec
exposes an inspectable path from historical evidence to explicit
preference claims and, subsequently, to the final recommendation.

5.7.4
Efficiency. Finally, we measure the computational overhead
introduced by the two-pass design on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000.
Table 10 compares direct recommendation with uncached and
cached versions of PROVE-Rec. Without caching, PROVE-Rec re-
quires two model passes and increases latency from approximately
65 ms to 313 ms per example. This overhead mainly comes from
generating the preference proof in Pass A. Because the proof only
needs to be updated when the user’s history changes, it can be
cached for repeated recommendation requests. In this setting, on-
line inference requires only Pass B and takes approximately 63 ms,
which is comparable to direct recommendation. Thus, the two-pass
reasoning procedure introduces additional cost when constructing
a new proof but little additional online latency when the proof can
be reused.
6
Conclusion
We presented PROVE-Rec, a framework for verifiable preference
reasoning in LLM-based recommendation. PROVE-Rec represents
reasoning as compact preference claims linked to selected historical
evidence and uses a proof-gated recommendation path to prevent
the model from bypassing the generated proof. Its verification ob-
jectives examine whether the selected evidence supports each claim
and whether the claim influences the target-item ranking, while a
preservation objective maintains recommendation quality under
the compact proof representation. Experiments on three review-rich
benchmark datasets show that PROVE-Rec improves recommen-
dation performance while achieving stronger evidence grounding
and decision influence than review-aware and reasoning-enhanced
baselines. These results suggest that recommendation reasoning
should be evaluated not only by whether it appears plausible, but
also by whether its evidence supports the stated preferences and
whether those preferences affect the final recommendation.
