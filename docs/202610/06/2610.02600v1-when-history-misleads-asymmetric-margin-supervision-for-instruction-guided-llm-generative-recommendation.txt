When History Misleads: Asymmetric Margin
Supervision for Instruction-Guided LLM
Generative Recommendation
Ming Yin1,2,∗, Yuhan Yang2, Chen Chen2, Xinyu Lin2, Wentao Shi2, Fangcong Yin2, Chaofei Yang2,
Chao Yang2, Jiyan Yang2, Hui Zhang2, Ning Jiang2, Yiran Chen1, Qifan Wang2
1Duke University, 2Meta
∗Work done during internship at Meta
In instruction-guided generative recommendation, LLM-based recommenders need to balance two
goals: responding to the user’s current request and aligning with the preferences in their interaction
history. When the two conflict, history events can override the request. We show that turning the effect
of individual history events into supervision faces two obstacles. First, the events that most influence
a recommendation are not necessarily the ones that support the target item. Second, removing a
misleading event can raise the target’s score but a competing item’s score even more, so a higher
target score alone does not guarantee a better ranking. We propose Asymmetric Intervention-Guided
Margin Supervision (AIMS), which converts the effect of removing individual history events into
ranking supervision. For training requests already ranked correctly, a frozen reference model identifies
request-specific deletions that improve both the target’s score and its margin over a competitor near
the recommendation cutoff. These margins serve as training targets, while the complete history is
retained as input. Training combines cross-entropy with an asymmetric auxiliary loss that penalizes
margin shortfalls and routes its gradient only through the competitor score. Inference is unchanged,
requiring no history editing or deletion search. Across six LLM backbones on an industrial dataset and
two public benchmarks, AIMS improves Recall and NDCG over strong baselines. Ablations support
request-specific margins and asymmetric supervision, and the selected deletions preferentially remove
constraint-violating history.

Introduction
Generative recommenders retrieve and rank items by decoding item identifiers autoregressively. In the
semantic-identifier (SID) design, each identifier is a short sequence of discrete codes derived from item
content (Rajput et al., 2023). Recent systems build on a pre-trained large language model (LLM) (Geng et al.,
2022; Bao et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024), whose ability to interpret natural language enables instruction-guided
recommendation (Zhang et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025), the setting of this work. Given a
natural-language request that states the user’s current intent and an interaction history that records past
behavior, the model should retrieve items that satisfy the request and match the user’s preferences. Ideally,
the two inputs play different roles: the request constrains what is acceptable now, and the history personalizes
the choice among acceptable items.
In practice, however, history events that conflict with the current request can override it. Suppose a user who
watched a cooking video of braised pork ribs asks for a quick vegetarian weeknight dinner, under 30 minutes
(Figure 2). The video suggests a taste for slow-cooked meat, so the model ranks a three-hour beef stew higher
than it would without the video, although the stew is neither vegetarian nor quick. Meanwhile, the same
video pushes down the 20-minute chickpea pasta the user wants (the target).
Existing approaches improve the use of interaction histories through counterfactual training and context-bias
mitigation (Zhang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026), edit or select history events before supplying them to the
model (Zhang et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023, 2024), or reduce output bias through preference optimization (Gao

et al., 2025). Editing a history can reduce the effect of an unhelpful event, but may also remove information
useful for personalizing the recommendation. We instead use the request-dependent effects of single history
events to define a score gap between the target and a competitor near the recommendation cutoff, which the
model learns from the complete history.
Using single-event interventions as supervision requires knowing which ones help, and an audit of frozen
recommenders (Section 2, Figure 1) reveals two obstacles. First, the magnitude of an event’s influence on the
prediction, a label-free quantity, does not determine whether the event supports or suppresses the target: the
most influential event in a history is not reliably more likely than a random one to support the target. We call
this separation between prediction influence and signed target support Influence–Utility Misalignment (IUM).
Second, a higher target score does not necessarily improve the recommendation: a deletion can raise the target
score while raising that of a competing item near the recommendation cutoff even more, reducing their score
gap (the target–competitor margin). These findings motivate two design choices. We evaluate interventions
offline, where the observed training target, unavailable at inference, is known and signed support can be
measured directly. We accept an intervention only if it improves both the target score and its margin over
the boundary competitor, treating this margin as a surrogate for, not a guarantee of, the decoded decision.
Selecting deletions by label-free influence, or by target uplift instead of margin gain, yields fewer eligible
deletions and lower Recall@10 (Section 4.3).
We therefore propose Asymmetric Intervention-Guided Margin Supervision (AIMS), in which a history deletion
defines the supervision, not the training input. A frozen reference model evaluates single-event deletions,
accepts a deletion only if it improves both the target score and the target–competitor margin, and caches
the largest accepted margin as a request-specific counterfactual margin. A student initialized from the same
checkpoint learns this margin from the complete history. Cross-entropy (CE) supervises the observed target on
all training requests, and an auxiliary loss, asymmetric competitor suppression (ACS), penalizes the student’s
margin when it falls below the counterfactual margin by more than a tolerance. This loss is asymmetric in its
gradient: since the target is already supervised directly, its score enters the forward margin but is detached, so
the auxiliary gradient passes only through the competitor score. It is also selective in scope: ACS applies only
to initially correct training requests, to help protect them from displacement during continued training, yet
the gains also extend to initially missed requests (Section 4.3). At inference, AIMS uses the original decoder
on the complete input, with no reference-model evaluation or deletion search.
In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
• Diagnosis. We document Influence–Utility Misalignment: a history event’s influence on the prediction does
not determine its signed support for the target. We also show that target-score gains alone do not determine
the decoded ranking.
• Method. AIMS converts instruction-conditioned history deletions into counterfactual margin targets and
penalizes margin shortfalls on complete histories through an asymmetric auxiliary loss.
• Evaluation. On an industrial dataset and two public benchmarks, AIMS improves Recall and NDCG over
strong baselines. Matched ablations support request-specific margins, margin-based deletion selection,
complete-history training, and ACS. On constraint-bearing training requests, the selected deletions remove
violating history events more often than expected by chance. A history-dropout control does not reproduce
the gains.

In the running example (Figure 2), masking the pork-ribs video changes the model’s prediction, but the size of

2.1
Request dependence of utility
Let θ0 denote a checkpoint obtained by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) a pre-trained LLM to predict target
SIDs given requests and histories. We keep this checkpoint frozen throughout the audits. For event hj, let Vj
be the history view obtained by intervening on that event while leaving all others unchanged. We define the
event’s utility for candidate v as
uj,v(q) = sθ0(v | q, H) −sθ0(v | q, Vj).
(2)
Positive utility means that retaining the event supports the candidate’s score; negative utility means that the
intervention raises it. For the observed target, we write Uj(q) = uj,y(q).
We first test whether utility depends on the request, with Vj masking hj. On the industrial audit set, we
compare three conditions per request: the factual request, an LLM-generated, meaning-preserving paraphrase
that controls for wording, and a generic request that omits the specific intent. Histories, targets, and masked
events are fixed, and full and masked scores are compared within each condition.
For both backbones, paraphrases preserve the factual utility ordering better than generic requests (Figure 1(a)).
The utility-sign disagreement rate with the factual condition is higher for generic requests than for paraphrases,
by 17.10 and 4.65 points for Llama and Gemma. Thus, an event’s utility for a fixed target depends on the
request, even with the history and reference model fixed.

2.2
Influence–utility misalignment
Identifying events that support the observed target requires knowing that target, which is unavailable at
inference. We therefore test whether prediction sensitivity, a label-free signal, can identify them instead.
We define the influence ρj(q) of hj as the Jensen–Shannon divergence between the full- and masked-history
first-token distributions, each normalized over valid first-level SID codes.
Influence is more strongly associated with utility magnitude than with signed utility (Figure 1(b)). Under
factual requests, its Spearman correlations with |Uj| are 0.300 for Llama and 0.167 for Gemma, compared
with 0.045 and 0.002 for Uj. Influence could still select supportive events, those whose target utility exceeds
an audit threshold of 10−4 nats, if the most influential events tended to support the target. Yet, compared
with uniform selection within each history, maximum-influence selection raises the supportive-event rate by
only 0.24 and 0.42 points for Llama and Gemma under factual requests, with both 95% intervals including
zero (Appendix C.7), and the point estimates turn negative under generic requests (−3.94 and −2.95).
We call this separation between prediction sensitivity and signed target support Influence–Utility Misalignment
(IUM). A signed attribution, the utility uj,ˆy for the top-1 prediction ˆy of θ0, does not resolve it. When ˆy is
not the target, uj,ˆy is weakly anti-correlated with Uj (Appendix C.7). Offline, however, the observed target
makes this signed score change measurable, so it can serve as supervision (Section 3).
2.3
From target scores to margins and decisions
Even with the observed target, a higher target score does not necessarily improve the recommendation, because
the same intervention can also raise competing items’ scores. For each request, we fix a competitor c at the
top-R boundary of the initial full-history beam: the first excluded candidate at rank R + 1 if the target is
already in the top-R list, and the candidate at rank R otherwise. Keeping its identity fixed across history
views, we define
mθ(y, c | q, V ) = sθ(y | q, V ) −sθ(c | q, V ),
(3)
∆jmθ0 = ∆jsy −∆jsc = −uj,y(q) + uj,c(q),
(4)
where ∆j denotes the intervened-view value minus the full-history value at θ0. The margin improves only
when the target uplift exceeds the competitor uplift. The same decomposition applies to score changes induced
by continued training.
The decoder audit deletes each history event of an initially missed request in turn (R = 10), with the deletion
operator of AIMS, and keeps the deletion with the largest target uplift if this uplift exceeds the audit threshold
(Figure 1(c)). We then measure its effect on the fixed competitor and the margin and re-decode the view.
Despite the target gain, the margin decreases for 11.8% (Llama) and 16.7% (Gemma) of the requests, and
re-decoding recovers only 1.08% and 1.50% of them as top-10 hits.
A fixed-competitor margin is pairwise: top-R membership also depends on other candidates and the beam-
search output, so the target can enter the decoded list even when this margin decreases (recoveries above the
diagonal in Figure 1(c)). We therefore check both target uplift and margin gain when constructing supervision,
with the margin as a surrogate for the decoded decision.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets and protocols.
We use an industrial dataset and two public benchmarks, Qilin (Chen et al., 2025b)
and KuaiSearch-Lite, the benchmark subset of the KuaiSearch e-commerce search dataset (Li et al., 2026).
The industrial dataset is sampled from anonymized production logs of a large-scale content platform; each
request is a natural-language user query, and the target is the item the user clicked. On it, every method
runs SID-constrained beam search over the full catalog. On the public benchmarks, every method ranks each
request’s released candidate pool by the teacher-forced SID log-probability (Eq. 1); pools with fewer than 40
candidates, too small for an informative top-10 cutoff, are extended with training-split impressions sampled
without click labels, identically for all methods (Appendix B).
Models and baselines.
On all three datasets we use six backbones from three families: Llama-3.1-8B/70B,
Gemma-3-4B/12B, and Qwen3-8B/14B. For each dataset and backbone, all methods start from the same SFT
checkpoint θ0. Continued CE continues CE-only training from θ0 and is the matched baseline for AIMS. We
also compare with CFT (Zhang et al., 2024), LETTER (RG) (Wang et al., 2024), LTRGR (Li et al., 2024),
and S-DPO (Chen et al., 2024), described in Appendix B. We report Recall@k and NDCG@k in percent,
with differences in percentage points and significance from paired user-cluster bootstrap tests.
4.2
Main Results
AIMS achieves the best Recall@10 and NDCG@10 for all six backbones on all three datasets (Tables 1 and 2).
Over the matched Continued CE baseline, it improves Recall@10 in every setting, by 4.0–10.9% in relative
terms. In repeated runs with three training seeds, it also exceeds both Continued CE and the strongest
baseline in every run (Table 8).

Ablation Studies and Analysis
Each ablation changes one design choice of AIMS and otherwise keeps its setup, protection set, and competitors;
all use Llama-3.1-8B and Gemma-3-12B on the industrial dataset and are evaluated on complete histories.
We report paired values as Llama/Gemma and state significance where tested (paired user-cluster bootstrap,
p < 0.05); other differences are point estimates.
Each request’s own gain matters.
Both the intervention gains and their assignment to requests contribute to
AIMS. R-comp, which removes the gains (mcf
i = m0
i ) and thus only keeps protected margins from falling more
than ε below those of θ0, still improves Recall@10 over Continued CE by +0.093/+0.336 (p < 0.05 on both
backbones), so the gains account for 71%/49% of the improvement of AIMS. ConstantGate replaces each
positive gain with its mean within the group of initially active or inactive requests, and Magnitude Shuffle
permutes positive gains within these groups; both keep which requests start active, yet fall between R-comp
and AIMS (Figure 3(a)), and AIMS exceeds Shuffle (p < 0.05 on both backbones), so the assignment itself
matters. AIMS also exceeds variants that derive gains from initial-margin difficulty (D-free), reassign its gains
by that difficulty (D-cal), or drop the target-uplift condition (Margin-only), and reassigning them by gains
computed under a generic request (Generic-matched) lowers both metrics, so the specific request matters
(Table 3, group A; Appendix B.5). Inserting a random item at each history position instead yields positive
gains about ten times smaller and only weakly correlated with the deletion gains (Appendix C.5).

5
Related Work
Personalized search asks when to rely on the user’s history (Dou et al., 2007; Ai et al., 2019), and Lu et al.
(2024) train LLM recommenders with category-based instructions and reinforcement learning to follow stated
intentions that conflict with past preferences. CFT and GDRT improve history use via counterfactual logit
comparisons and group-robust training (Zhang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026); CauseRec, STEAM, and
ReLLa synthesize, correct, or select the input history (Zhang et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023, 2024); and retrieval-
augmented generation trains retrievers and inference-time filters on label-dependent utility (Izacard et al.,
2023; Wang et al., 2023). LETTER, LTRGR, S-DPO, and OneRec use ranking or preference losses (Wang
et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2025), and Margin-MSE distills teacher score
margins (Hofstätter et al., 2020). AIMS instead turns single-event deletions into request-specific margin
targets while keeping complete histories in training and inference, penalizing shortfalls via the competitor
only (Appendix E).
6
Conclusion
We documented influence–utility misalignment, showed that target-score gains need not improve rankings, and
proposed AIMS, which turns request-specific history deletions into counterfactual margin targets learned on
complete histories through an asymmetric auxiliary loss, leaving inference unchanged. Across six backbones
and three datasets, AIMS achieves the best Recall@10 and NDCG@10 in every setting, improving Recall@10
over matched Continued CE by 4.0–10.9% (relative), with larger gains under instruction–history conflict that
dropping history during training does not reproduce. The deletions behind its margin targets preferentially
remove history that violates the request.

B
Experimental Details
B.1
Training Pipeline
Semantic identifiers.
In all experiments, the decoder predicts three SID tokens, each from a level-specific
vocabulary of 2,048 codes. For the industrial dataset, we use the first three levels of the platform’s production
semantic identifiers, so that all datasets share the same SID format. For Qilin and KuaiSearch-Lite, we train
a separate three-level RQ-VAE over Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B representations of the item text. SID mappings
are dataset-specific and fixed across all methods and backbones within each dataset, and the 6,144 SID tokens
are added to each backbone’s vocabulary.
Training stages.
For each dataset and backbone, the base model is first trained to generate item SIDs and
then fine-tuned with CE on (request, history, target) instances, which gives θ0. Continued CE, all baselines,
and AIMS start from θ0 with the same instances, batch size, and number of steps.
Compute.
All experiments were run on a multi-node cluster with eight NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs per node.
B.2
Datasets
Industrial dataset.
The industrial dataset is sampled from anonymized production logs of a large-scale
content platform. Each request is a natural-language user query, the target is the item the user clicked for that
request, and the history is the user’s 30 most recent interactions before the request, represented by their SIDs.
Training uses three consecutive days of logs and evaluation a single later day, separated by a one-day gap.
Qilin.
Qilin (Chen et al., 2025b) contains app-level sessions with real Chinese queries, the notes exposed for
each request, click feedback, and up to 20 recently clicked notes. We use its search subset. For each request,
the query is q, the released pre-request click sequence is H, and Y is the set of distinct exposed notes that
received a click. We retain requests with Y̸ = ∅, expand each y ∈Y into one training instance that keeps the
request identifier, and compute Recall@k and NDCG@k at the request level over all positives in Y. We keep
the official test split (6,192 requests) and divide the 44,024 released training requests into 39,931 training and
4,093 development requests with a deterministic user-disjoint hash split.
15


KuaiSearch-Lite.
KuaiSearch (Li et al., 2026) contains real e-commerce search logs from the Kuaishou
platform, with authentic queries, the items exposed for each request, click and purchase feedback, and item
metadata including titles, brands, sellers, and three-level category hierarchies. We use KuaiSearch-Lite, the
released subset that keeps the complete search and interaction records of 102,086 randomly sampled users
over one month (555,553 queries). For each request, the query is q, H contains up to the 20 most recent
clicked items provided by the ranking table, and Y is the set of distinct clicked items. Requests are filtered,
expanded, and evaluated as on Qilin. Following the benchmark’s temporal protocol, we use the last day of
logs as the test split and partition the remaining requests into user-disjoint training and development sets,
which yields 257,780 / 28,781 / 8,456 click-positive training / development / test requests.
B.3
Evaluation Protocols
Full-catalog retrieval.
On the industrial dataset, every method runs SID-constrained beam search (beam size
100) over the full catalog, which returns a ranked list of 100 unique valid SIDs per request; Recall@k and
NDCG@k use the top k of this list. Recall@k is the fraction of test requests whose target appears in the top
k, and NDCG@k uses binary relevance with one target per request.
Candidate-pool ranking.
On the public benchmarks, each candidate in the pool of a request is scored by the
full-sequence SID log-probability of Eq. (1) under teacher forcing, on the complete request and history, and
the pool is sorted; the released order breaks exact ties. All clicked items of a request are relevant: Recall@k is
the fraction of them in the top k, and NDCG@k uses binary gains with the ideal ranking placing all of them
first. Sampled-candidate metrics can deviate from full-ranking metrics (Krichene and Rendle, 2020), so we
use them only to compare methods on identical pools; absolute values are not comparable across datasets.
Pool extension.
Some released impression pools contain fewer candidates than the evaluation cutoff, making
Recall@10 uninformative. We therefore deterministically extend pools containing fewer than 40 items with
distinct items sampled from training-split impressions, while retaining all logged candidates and leaving
larger pools unchanged. Sampling is seeded by the request identifier and does not use click labels; existing
candidates, including all clicked items, are excluded from sampling. This rule is fixed across splits, and every
method is evaluated on identical pools.
AIMS with candidate pools.
Each clicked item of a request forms one training instance. For an instance, the
initial list of Section 3.1 is the pool sorted by the scores of θ0 with the other clicked items of the same request
removed: the instance enters the protection set when its target is among the ten highest-scored candidates of
this list, and its competitor is the eleventh candidate. Inference is unchanged for all methods.
Significance testing.
All significance statements use a paired user-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples:
users are resampled with replacement, all test requests of a user are kept together, every method is evaluated
on the same resamples, and a difference is called significant (p < 0.05) when its two-sided 95% percentile
interval excludes zero.
Baselines.
All baselines start from θ0 and share AIMS’s SIDs, positive training instances, training schedule,
and evaluation protocol. Hyperparameters are tuned on the same validation split. Continued CE performs
additional CE-only training. CFT (Zhang et al., 2024) adds a counterfactual CE loss on the difference between
full-history and history-absent vocabulary logits at teacher-forced target positions. LETTER (RG) (Wang
et al., 2024) applies its temperature-scaled ranking-guided generation loss to our shared SIDs. LTRGR (Li et al.,
2024) combines generation and margin-based ranking losses using SID sequence scores over model-retrieved
candidates. S-DPO (Chen et al., 2024) applies a multi-negative softmax preference loss to reference-relative
SID sequence log-probabilities, with θ0 as the frozen reference.

Training seeds
Continued CE, the strongest baseline of each backbone, and AIMS are each trained with the same three seeds
from the same θ0. The seed affects only the randomness of continued training. The cached competitors and
margins of AIMS are computed once from θ0 and are identical across seeds. Table 8 reports means, standard
deviations, and paired differences over seeds. All other results in the paper use one of these seeds, the same
for every experiment.
C.3
Deletion budget and competitor set
Table 9 varies the deletion budget and the competitor set around the defaults (K = 1, M = 1). For K > 1, a
non-stopping greedy search evaluates, in each round, all one-event extensions of the previous round’s raw-
margin-gain maximizer, even when the best intermediate gain is non-positive. The same eligibility conditions
are then applied, and the eligible set with the largest margin gain is selected. At K = 3, Conservative selection
instead takes the smallest eligible set that attains at least a fraction η = 0.9 of the maximum gain. With
M > 1, Pair-mean averages ACS losses over the first M excluded candidates, and Set-max compares against
the highest-scoring candidate in this fixed set. Larger budgets give small, backbone-dependent changes with
up to 2.9× as many scoring views. Pair-mean changes are small and inconsistent, and Set-max degrades as M
grows on both backbones.

C.4
History use
Request-only CE continues CE training from θ0 with the history field left empty, using the same training
requests, targets, and schedule as Continued CE. History dropout uses the same setup but empties the history
of each training instance with probability 0.5, drawn independently per instance from a stream fixed by the
training seed, so it lies halfway between Continued CE (p = 0) and Request-only CE (p = 1); we fix p = 0.5
in advance rather than tuning it, and inference uses the complete history. For the history swap, we replace
each request’s history with three histories of other users that match its request category, history length, and
coarse category composition. The replacements are chosen without targets or model outputs and shared by
all methods, and the three results are averaged per request. Groups follow Appendix D. Table 10 reports the
results.
C.5

histories have the maximum length of 30, most views contain 31 events, one more than the longest training
history. Inserted items are sampled from the training-set frequency of history items, excluding the user’s
history, the target, and the competitor, with seeds fixed by request and position. We score the target and

Retention–recovery decomposition
On the industrial dataset, each test request has one target, and all methods are evaluated on the same split,
defined by the top-10 decisions of θ0. The Recall@10 difference between two methods therefore decomposes
exactly as ∆R@10 = p0 ∆Ret + (1 −p0) ∆Rec, where ∆denotes AIMS minus Continued CE and p0 is the
test hit rate of θ0 (6.70/7.70% for Llama-3.1-8B/Gemma-3-12B). With the retention and recovery rates of
Figure 4, the share of the gain due to higher recovery, (1 −p0) ∆Rec/∆R@10, is 68/85%.

We analyze the industrial test requests (25% of the test set) that state an explicit positive content-category

Severity trend.
Table 13 reports the results by stratum, and Figure 5(a,b) plots them. Continued CE’s
FAIL rate increases monotonically with κi, indicating that the frozen history statistic captures how strongly
historical evidence points away from the current constraint. AIMS’s absolute and relative violation reductions
also increase monotonically. From κi = 0 to κi ≥0.8, the absolute reduction grows from 0.6 to 8.6 points on
Llama and from 0.5 to 8.4 points on Gemma; the highest- and lowest-severity intervals do not overlap. The
point estimates of the Recall@10 gains are non-decreasing over the same strata, so there is no evidence in
these estimates that the compliance improvement trades off against target retrieval. In point estimates, D-cal
improves over Continued CE in every stratum but remains below AIMS, with the gap widening as conflict
severity increases. These are observational strata. The monotone association does not by itself identify conflict
severity as the causal source of the gains.
Labels of selected deletions.
We test whether the deletions selected by AIMS remove history events that
violate the request’s constraint. We apply the same parser and frozen metadata to the protected training
requests of the industrial dataset and label the history event removed by each selected deletion (Eq. (8));
these labels are used only for this analysis. We keep requests with an explicit category constraint that meet
Eq. (10), have a pass target, and contain both pass and fail history events (0 < κi < 1). If the selection
ignored the labels, it would remove a fail event with probability Fi/ni, where ni counts all single-event
deletions of request i (unknown events stay in the denominator). Table 14 and Figure 5(c) compare the
observed share with the mean of this probability. To compare with the generic request of Section 2.1, the first
two rows use the requests with an eligible deletion under both requests, so they share N and the expected
share. The selected deletion removes a fail event 16.8/16.6 points more often than expected. Under the
generic request, with labels still taken from the factual constraint, the excess falls to 2.9/4.1 points. When
the target itself violates the constraint, fail events are removed 12.3/11.1 points less often than expected.
The labels do not enter AIMS; they only describe which events its selection removes.

Request–history conflict.
Studies of personalized web search found that personalization helps queries with
ambiguous intent but has little effect, or even hurts, when the intent is clear (Dou et al., 2007; Teevan et al.,
2008). Personalized product search combines the query with the user’s history (Ai et al., 2017); the zero
attention model attends over the user’s history conditioned on the query and can attend to none of it (Ai
et al., 2019), and denoising attention has been proposed for query-aware user modeling (Bassani et al., 2024).
Among LLM recommenders, Lu et al. (2024) train the model to follow the stated intention when it conflicts
with preferences inferred from the history, using control instructions synthesized from item categories with
labels from a sequential recommender, followed by reinforcement learning; our requests are instead real user
queries with observed click targets. We therefore treat that pipeline as complementary to AIMS rather than
as a directly comparable baseline. In LLMs more broadly, models may follow either the supplied context
or their parametric knowledge when the two conflict (Longpre et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2024), context-aware
decoding contrasts predictions with and without the context at inference (Shi et al., 2024), and the instruction
hierarchy trains models to favor higher-priority instructions (Wallace et al., 2024). These works analyze or
control how much a model relies on the history or context, through its architecture, decoding, or training
data. AIMS instead evaluates individual history events offline under the current request, using the observed
target, and turns their effects into training supervision, leaving the architecture and inference unchanged.
Label-dependent context utility as supervision.
Retrieval-augmented generation also derives supervision from
label-dependent estimates of context utility. Atlas trains its retriever to predict how much each retrieved
document improves the likelihood of the output, and also evaluates a leave-one-out objective based on how
much the prediction degrades when one retrieved document is removed (Izacard et al., 2023). FILCO labels
useful context spans with label-dependent criteria, including string inclusion, lexical overlap, and the ratio
of gold-output probabilities with and without a span as the supplied context; it then trains a filter that
runs without gold outputs at inference and supplies the filtered context to the generator (Wang et al., 2023).
Because filtering can also discard relevant passages, Yoran et al. (2024) instead train the reader on mixed
relevant and irrelevant contexts. AIMS also measures signed utility with the label: in our audit (Section 2),
the tested label-free influence measure is not a reliable proxy for signed target support. AIMS differs in what
it intervenes on and how it uses the result. It deletes individual events of a user’s history under the current
request, and it turns their effects into a margin target for the recommender itself rather than training a
retriever or filter or editing the input, so the recommender keeps the complete history during training and
24


inference. Training on the same deletion views instead is less effective (Section 4.3). Every model in that
comparison uses the complete history at inference, so it concerns how deletions enter training. A filter trained
on our deletion labels and applied at inference would instead add a selection step to deployment, and it would
have to predict target-dependent effects without the target (Table 14).
History synthesis, selection, and correction.
CauseRec scores concepts relative to the next-item target and
replaces indispensable or dispensable concepts to construct contrastive user-sequence representations (Zhang
et al., 2021). It therefore provides a precedent for target-aware history transformations used during training.
STEAM learns keep, delete, and insert operations and supplies corrected sequences to its recommender (Lin
et al., 2023). In candidate-conditioned click-through-rate prediction, ReLLa retrieves semantically relevant
historical interactions and mixes retrieved and original views during tuning (Lin et al., 2024). In full-catalog
generative retrieval there is no candidate item at inference, so ReLLa would have to retrieve history by
the request, an inference-time selection step like the filters discussed above. These approaches improve
recommendation through sequence synthesis, correction, or selection. AIMS evaluates the contribution of
historical evidence to the current target–competitor comparison. An accepted deletion need not identify an
erroneous interaction: the recorded behavior may be valid while contributing adversely under the present
instruction.
Context and output debiasing in LLM recommenders.
CFT combines standard likelihood training with a
target-label objective on the difference between full-history and history-absent logits (Zhang et al., 2024). Its
counterfactual signal is thus label-directed, rather than an unsigned sensitivity objective. GDRT groups target-
token occurrences according to their predictability without user history and applies group-distributionally
robust tuning (Wang et al., 2026). This changes training emphasis across groups, whereas AIMS constructs a
margin target for an individual request. SPRec addresses output bias through self-play: observed interactions
provide positive responses, previous model predictions provide negatives, and SFT alternates with DPO (Gao
et al., 2025). AIMS instead keeps its initially decoded competitor fixed throughout continued training. At
decoding time, D3 addresses amplification and homogeneity by removing length normalization associated with
nearly deterministic tokens and incorporating scores from an auxiliary recommender (Bao et al., 2024). AIMS
retains the original SID-constrained deployment decoder.
Ranking objectives and asymmetric negative supervision.
Relative item preference is central to recommenda-
tion objectives such as BPR (Rendle et al., 2009). In generative retrieval, GR2 incorporates graded relevance
through identifier design and constrained contrastive learning (Tang et al., 2024). Among our baselines,
LETTER (RG) applies LETTER’s temperature-scaled ranking-guided generation loss (Wang et al., 2024),
and LTRGR adds a learning-to-rank phase with a rank loss over retrieved candidates (Li et al., 2024). OneRec
applies iterative preference alignment to generated recommendation sessions, using a reward model to select
preferred and rejected outputs (Deng et al., 2025). For language-model alignment, DPO optimizes response
pairs using reference-relative sequence probabilities, while SimPO uses length-normalized likelihoods and
a target reward margin (Rafailov et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2024). S-DPO, another baseline, extends DPO
to multiple negatives for LM-based recommenders (Chen et al., 2024). Unlikelihood training complements
likelihood maximization with penalties on undesirable outputs (Welleck et al., 2020). These objectives shape
the ranked list, preferences between outputs, or the likelihood of undesired outputs in general. AIMS instead
derives its comparison threshold from reference margins, strengthened by eligible history interventions, and
gates its auxiliary penalty by the student’s margin shortfall.
Recent ACPO likewise uses asymmetric preference optimization, retaining the chosen-response gradient while
adaptively scaling the rejected-response gradient (Huang et al., 2026). AIMS instead stops the target branch
of its auxiliary hinge and updates the competitor branch when the constraint is active. Routing the auxiliary
gradient through both scores, as symmetric pairwise losses do, lowers Recall@10 relative to competitor-only

Transferring supervision and preserving recommendations.
Learning with different teacher and student inputs
connects AIMS to generalized distillation (Lopez-Paz et al., 2016) and context distillation, which transfers
benefits obtained from a teacher’s context into a model evaluated with a different context (Snell et al., 2022).
More directly, Margin-MSE trains neural rankers to match cached teacher score differences between document
pairs (Hofstätter et al., 2020). AIMS also transfers a scalar comparison, but strengthens it with eligible history
deletions and treats it as a minimum desired margin with a tolerance, rather than an equality target.
The focus on initially correct requests connects to the broader goal of retaining existing capabilities during
adaptation, as in Learning without Forgetting (Li and Hoiem, 2016). AIMS operates within one recommenda-
tion task, with the original training requests available. Its reference supplies a selected target–competitor
comparison, which an eligible intervention can strengthen. This selective constraint complements CE without
requiring the student to reproduce the reference’s entire output distribution. A KL penalty toward the reference
model, as in the KL-regularized objective underlying DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), is the distribution-level form
of such preservation, and our S-DPO baseline optimizes a preference loss derived from this objective with θ0 as
the frozen reference (Appendix B). R-comp, which sets mcf
i = m0
i , is the margin-level form of this preservation
within AIMS: it improves Recall@10 over Continued CE by +0.093/+0.336, and the request-specific gains
account for the remaining 71%/49% of the improvement of AIMS (Section 4.3).
Attribution and counterfactual explanations.
The audit of Section 2 measures how a prediction changes
when a history event is removed, which relates it to attribution. Quantifying a feature’s contribution through
removal is a well-established explanation strategy (Covert et al., 2021). Additive attribution methods such as
SHAP also assign signed contributions to individual predictions (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). Influence functions
instead trace predictions to training examples through approximate changes in model parameters (Koh
and Liang, 2017). ACCENT extends this approach to the score difference between a recommended item
and a replacement, searching for a small set of historical training interactions whose removal would reverse
the recommendation (Tran et al., 2021). Its intervention concerns estimated retraining effects, whereas
AIMS directly evaluates input deletions with a frozen model and uses their label-directed improvements as
supervision. Our diagnostic term influence specifically denotes target-free distributional sensitivity, distinct
from the signed influence-function quantities used in this literature. The utility readout is a predictive contrast
under a specified intervention, rather than an identified causal effect of a user’s behavior in the world.
