Abstract 

Generative recommendation (GR) represents items with semantic IDs (i.e., discrete token sequences) and generates target item tokens as recommendations. Despite its promising results, existing methods predominantly opti-mize for accuracy while neglecting the cred-ibility of the recommendations they generate. This oversight inevitably exposes users to un-credible content (e.g., fake news) with serious societal consequences, including user distrust, reputation harm to platforms, and broader so-cial instability. To address this critical yet un-derexplored challenge, we propose CreGR , the first credible GR model that jointly tackles con-tent credibility across the two core stages of GR: tokenization and generation . In the tok-enization stage, we design a new credibility-aware tokenizer that explicitly encourages the model to learn discriminative tokens respec-tively for credible and uncredible items, thereby disentangling credibility signals at the token level. Building on this, in the generation stage, we propose a novel accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generator grounded in discrete diffusion. Specifically, we introduce an asymmetric masking probability reduction strategy that selectively diminishes the con-tribution of tokens associated with uncredi-ble content to the generation process, while leaving tokens encoding user preference sig-nals unaffected so as to preserve recommen-dation accuracy. Experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of 

CreGR . The implementation code and prepro-cessed datasets of our method are provided in 

https://github.com/iamZhuoCai/CreGR .

Generative recommendation (GR) has emerged as a transformative paradigm for recommender systems in today’s generative AI era, reformulat-ing the recommendation problem from conven-

Compared to traditional methods, GR offers superior generalization that subsumes retrieval and ranking into a single generation pro-cess. Despite its remarkable success and rapid adoption by major platforms serving hundreds of millions of users (Deng et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2024; He et al., 2026; Penha et al., 2025), existing GR methods are predominantly optimized for rec-ommendation accuracy while neglecting a critical yet underexplored societal dimension: the content credibility of the generated recommendations. As illustrated in Figure 1, existing GR methods may accurately capture user preference yet recommend uncredible content (e.g., fake news) to users. This oversight leads to serious consequences, including eroded user trust, reputational risks to platforms, and even social instability (Cai et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2024b, 2022b). There are some recent efforts on developing cred-ible content recommendation methods (Wang et al., arXiv:2610.05670v1 [cs.IR] 5 Oct 2026 2022c, 2024a; Cai et al., 2026a,b; Sallami et al., 2023). For example, some works (Wang et al., 2022c, 2024a) disentangle news content into ve-racity embeddings and event embeddings, and uti-lize only event embeddings to compute user-item matching scores, thereby mitigating the influence of uncredible content. Cai et al. (Cai et al., 2026a) employ a diffusion model to improve recommen-dation credibility. However, these methods all op-erate within the embedding matching paradigm, where recommendations are made by computing similarity scores between user and item embed-dings over a large candidate pool. As such, they are inherently incompatible with the emerging GR paradigm, which forgoes embedding matching en-tirely and instead directly generates the semantic IDs (SIDs) of target items. In this paper, we focus on how to improve the credibility of GR. However, building a credible GR model is non-trivial, primar-ily due to the following two challenges (CHs): 

CH1: How to learn discriminative semantic IDs for credible and uncredible content items in the tokenization stage? Existing methods to-kenize items by encoding their metadata (e.g., ti-tles and descriptions) through pre-trained language models and subsequently quantizing the resulting embeddings into discrete tokens (i.e., SIDs) via vec-tor quantization (e.g., RQ-VAE (Lee et al., 2022)). However, these models are pre-trained with objec-tives such as masked language modeling or next-token prediction, which capture topical and syntac-tic patterns but are inherently agnostic to content credibility (Chuang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2022). As a result, credible and uncredible content that share similar topics are mapped to nearby regions in the embedding space. This credibility entangle-ment causes topically similar but factually diver-gent items to be assigned similar/nearby tokens, making them difficult to discriminate during the subsequent generation process and potentially lead-ing to the recommendation of uncredible content. 

CH2: How to balance recommendation accu-racy and credibility in the generation stage? Astraightforward solution to mitigate uncredible con-tent is to directly remove uncredible items from training data and retrain the generation model. However, this naive removal strategy inevitably de-grades recommendation accuracy, as the valuable information (e.g., topics) in the uncredible items that reflect users’ preference is also removed. For instance, a user who frequently engages with un-credible health news articles still reveals a strong in-terest in wellness topics, which should be leveraged to capture the user’s preference and support accu-rate generation. The empirical evidence is provided in Appendix C. Therefore, there is a fundamental trade-off between recommendation accuracy and credibility in the generation stage. To address these challenges, in this pa-per, we propose a novel credible generative recommendation model, CreGR . Specifically, to overcome CH1 , we design a novel credibility-aware tokenizer that introduces credibility regu-larization to enforce intra-class compactness and inter-class separation between credible and uncred-ible items. This compels the codebook to dis-entangle credibility signals at the discrete token level and thereby eliminating entanglement be-tween factually divergent items. To address CH2 ,we propose an accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generator grounded in discrete diffusion. Exploiting the fact that discrete diffusion training is conditioned on masked tokens, we design an asymmetric masking probability reduction strategy that selectively reduces the masking probabilities of tokens associated with uncredible content iden-tified in the tokenization stage, while leaving all other tokens encoding valuable user preference sig-nals at their original masking probabilities. We instantiate this strategy through two variants: a 

hard weighting strategy that performs absolute erasure of uncredible token contributions, and a 

soft weighting strategy that applies distance-aware down-weighting based on proximity to cluster cen-troids. These designs gracefully suppress uncredi-ble generation while preserving the topical prefer-ence signals, achieving a balance between recom-mendation accuracy and credibility. Overall, our contributions are summarized as: • We propose a novel credible generative content recommendation model, CreGR , which is the first GR model that considers the credibility of the generated recommendations. • We design a credibility-aware tokenizer and a novel accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generator, which work collaboratively to improve the recommendation credibility while preserving high recommendation accuracy. • Extensive experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of our pro-posed model in generating credible and accurate content recommendations. 2 Problem Formulation 

2.1 Credible Content Recommendation 

Following prior work (Wang et al., 2022c, 2024a; Cai et al., 2026a), we formulate credible content recommendation as the task of reducing user expo-sure to uncredible items, such as fake news and misinformation, which degrade user experience and pose adverse societal impacts. Credible con-tent recommendation follows the task of sequen-tial recommendation (Wang et al., 2021, 2019a,b, 2018; Cai et al., 2025). A recommendation model is deemed more credible when its generated recom-mendation lists contain fewer such items. Besides, this task requires developing a recom-mendation model that inherently ensures the credi-bility, rather than relying on post-hoc filtering (i.e., removing identified uncredible items from final recommendation lists). Post-hoc filtering treats the symptom rather than the cause: the underlying model has already learned latent patterns favoring uncredible content during training, and such filter-ing remains vulnerable to adversarial manipulation where uncredible content is crafted to evade detec-tion (Zhou and Zafarani, 2020). 

Definition 1 Content credibility. Content credibil-ity is defined as the trustworthiness of the infor-mation conveyed by an item. An item is regarded as uncredible if it contains false, misleading, or inaccurate information (e.g., fake news, health mis-information), and credible otherwise. 

2.2 Generative Recommendation 

Given a user’s interaction history H =

{v1, v 2, . . . , v n−1}, GR formulates the recommen-dation task as a token generation problem to gen-erate the tokens of the target item vn that users may be interested in. Each item vi is repre-sented as a sequence of discrete tokens (i.e., SIDs) 

[ci, 1, c i, 2, . . . , c i,L ] using vector quantization tech-niques (e.g., RQ-VAE), where L denotes the num-ber of quantization levels. Afterwards, the user’s interaction sequence can be represented as:

(1) GR aims to learn a probabilistic model Pθ that generates the tokens of the target item vn =[cn, 1, · · · , c n,L ] conditioned on SH: Pθ(vn|S H).Existing GR models can be categorized into autoregressive (AR) models that generate the to-kens of target items from left-to-right, and non-autoregressive (NAR) models that generate tokens in parallel via discrete diffusion. Our proposed method is based on the second category. 

NAR GR. This paradigm generates the tokens of the target item in parallel via discrete diffusion modeling (Shi et al., 2025). In the reverse process, the model Pθ predicts all masked tokens simultane-ously. Specifically, the reverse process proceeds over T steps. 3 Methodology 

As shown in Figure 2, CreGR comprises two key components. (1) Credibility-aware tokenizer 

discretizes each item into token sequences while enforcing discriminative representations between credible and uncredible items at the token level. (2) Accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generator reduces the masking probabilities of tokens encoding uncredible information identified by the tokenizer, while leaving all other tokens unaffected, thereby suppressing the influence of un-credible content in the generation process without compromising recommendation accuracy.

into a title embedding etit i and a description em-bedding edes i . 3.2 Accuracy-preserving and Credibility-oriented Generator 

To address CH2 , we propose an accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generator based on discrete diffusion. Specifically, we design an 

asymmetric masking probability reduction strat-egy to suppress the contributions of the last tokens of uncredible items during generation, while pre-serving valuable content-level topical signals. Our design is motivated by the hierarchical na-ture of our credibility-aware tokenizer: the ear-lier levels capture dominant semantic patterns such as topics and writing styles, while the later levels progressively encode finer-grained details through residual refinement (Rajput et al., 2023). Since fac-tual discrepancies between credible and uncredible items typically manifest as subtle, detail-level dif-ferences (e.g., specific claims, cited sources) rather than coarse topical divergence (Zhou and Zafarani, 2020; Zhou et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2026c; Yang et al., 2023a), such credibility-discriminative sig-nals are more likely to be preserved at the last few token levels after the shared topical information has been absorbed by the preceding levels. Conversely, reducing the masking probabilities of earlier levels would suppress tokens that encode valuable user preference signals, thereby degrading recommen-dation accuracy. Empirical evidence supporting this design choice is provided in Appendix E. Specifically, our generator operates through a forward masking process and a reverse denoising process. 4.1 Datasets and Evaluation Metrics Datasets. Following prior work (Cai et al., 2026a), we conduct experiments on three real-world datasets: PolitiFact (Shu et al., 2020), (Shu et al., 2020), and MHMisinfo (Nguyen et al., 2025). The PolitiFact and GossipCop datasets are derived from FakeNewsNet repository 1 (Shu et al., 2020). PolitiFact and GossipCop datasets are user-news interaction datasets, where the uncredible items are fake news. MHMisinfo is a user-health information interaction dataset derived from a video-based men-tal health misinformation dataset 2 (Nguyen et al., 2025), where the uncredible items are health misin-formation. To the best of our knowledge, these are the only publicly available datasets that satisfy both requirements of this task : user-item inter-action data and item-level credibility labels. The statistics of these datasets are reported in Table 1. 

Evaluation Metrics. We adopt widely used met-rics across three dimensions: recommendation ac-curacy (NDCG@ N , HR@ N ), content credibil-ity (CR@ N , i.e., credible rate, the proportion of credible items in the recommendation list), and their balance (HC@ N , which combines HR@ N

and CR@ N ) (Cai et al., 2026a). All metrics are higher-the-better , and their detailed formulations are provided in Appendix B.2.

4.3 Implementation Details 

For fair comparison, all baseline methods are ini-tialized with hyperparameters suggested in their original papers and subsequently fine-tuned on our datasets to ensure their best performances are re-ported. We first train the tokenization stage with 10k epochs, and the learning rate is searched in range {1e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4}. We then train the gen-eration stage and set the maximum epoch to 500, with learning rate searched in range {5e-4, 1e-3, 3e-3, 5e-3}. Batch size is searched in range {256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096}. L, K, T are set to 4, 256 and 4 following prior work (Shi et al., 2025). λ is searched in range {3, 4, 5, 6, 7}. Beam width is fixed to 50. Model parameters are optimized us-ing AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017). Each method is run five times, and the average perfor-mance is reported. More implementation details are provided in Appendix B.3.

From the results reported in Table 2, we have the following observations: 

Credibility Improvement. CreGR achieves the best recommendation credibility (CR@5 and CR@10) across all datasets, demonstrating the ef-fectiveness of our credibility-aware tokenization and accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generation designs. Notably, the hard weighting strategy (i.e., CreGR (hard)) attains higher credi-bility than its soft counterpart (i.e., CreGR (hard)), owing to its more aggressive suppression of uncred-ible token contributions. 

Accuracy Preservation. CreGR preserves compet-itive recommendation accuracy. Although CreGR 

yields slightly lower NDCG than Disco on Politi-Fact and MHMisinfo datasets, it maintains superior or comparable accuracy across all other cases. This can be attributed to: (1) our accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generator selectively re-duces the masking probability of only the last to-ken of uncredible items, leaving all other tokens that encode user preference signals unaffected; and (2) the incorporation of collaborative embeddings from a pretrained model further enriches item rep-resentations, providing additional accuracy gains. 

Credibility-accuracy Balance. CreGR achieves the best performance in terms of HC@5 and HC@10, demonstrating its effectiveness in bal-ancing recommendation accuracy and credibility. This achievement can be attributed to our designed accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented gener-ator which can mitigates uncredible content while preserving recommendation accuracy as much as possible. This balance is of particular practical sig-nificance, as both objectives are indispensable in real-world recommendation scenarios. (3) T . HC@5 consistently rises as T increases. Re-ducing the number of generation steps significantly increases the uncertainty of generation trajectory, and therefore decrease recommendation accuracy and credibility. Larger T ensures finer-grained gen-eration trajectories, leading to optimal recommen-dation performance. 4.6.3 Label-noise Performance 

In our Limitations section, we point out that explor-ing semi-supervised or unsupervised approaches is part of our future work, since in real-world sce-narios, items’ credibility labels may not be always available. These can serve as the solutions when credibility labels are noise or sparse. In the current version, we do not explicitly design a mechanism to resolve the label-noise issue. However, we con-ducted additional experiments to investigate the performance of CreGR in label-noise scenarios. Specifically, we randomly flipped the labels of a portion (5%, 10%, and 15%) of uncredible items during training (at test time, correct labels are used to accurately evaluate model performance). As shown in Table 5, CreGR also achieves supe-rior results in the label-noise setting. Specifically, when the noise level is raised to 15%, the recom-mendation credibility (CR@5) of CreGR only drops slightly, from 0.9855 to 0.9780, still remaining at a very high level (significantly outperforming LET-TER’s 0.9080). This indicates that although with-out specific designs, our method still have high po-tential to achieve great recommendation accuracy and credibility in scenarios where partial labels are noise or unavailable.

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose a novel credible gener-ative recommendation model, CreGR , to mitigate the recommendations of uncredible content in RSs. Specifically, we design a credibility-aware tok-enizer to learn discriminative item tokens via cred-ibility regularization. Afterwards, we propose a novel accuracy-preserving and credibility-oriented generator to reduce the contributions of tokens en-coding uncredible information in the generation process. During this process, tokens representing users’ preference signals remain unaffected to pre-serve high recommendation accuracy. Extensive experiments on three real-world datasets demon-strate the effectiveness of CreGR on improving rec-ommendation credibility while preserving high rec-ommendation accuracy. Limitations 

While our proposed model demonstrates promising results in generating credible content recommen-dations, there are several limitations that warrant further investigation. 

Label availability. Our model relies on the avail-ability of labeled data to identify credible and un-credible items during the tokenization and genera-tion stage. In real-world scenarios, such labels may not always be available or may be noisy, which could affect the performance of our model. We plan to explore semi-supervised or unsupervised ap-proaches to mitigate this limitation in future work. For example, Large Language Models (LLMs) (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2026) and LLM agents (Huang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2026a,b,c) can serve as effective tools to handle semi-supervised and unsupervised scenarios, due to their powerful open-world knowledge. 

Content modality. Our current model primar-ily focuses on textual content (e.g., news arti-cles). Although MHMisinfo is a user-video in-teraction dataset, it doesn’t provide the video meta-data. It only contains the video titles and descrip-tions. However, in many recommendation scenar-ios, items may contain multimodal content (e.g., images, videos) that also contribute to their cred-ibility. Future work could explore extending our model to incorporate multimodal information for a more comprehensive assessment of item credi-bility via multimodal LLMs (Li et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025, 2026a,c,b) or other techniques. 

Dataset Scalability. The datasets used in our experiments (GossipCop: 510,149 sequences; Poli-tiFact: 103,335 sequences) contain substantially more interaction sequences than commonly used Amazon benchmarks in GR (McAuley et al., 2015; Hou et al., 2024) (Sports: 35,598; Beauty: 22,363; Toys: 19,412), with GossipCop even containing ap-proximately 2-3 times more sequences than larger Amazon datasets (Automotive: 193,651; Phones: 157,212; Tools: 240,799). This indicates that our used datasets provide a robust basis for validat-ing the effectiveness and generalizability of CreGR .However, we acknowledge that the item vocabu-laries of these datasets are relatively limited, as they represent the only publicly available bench-marks that satisfy the dual requirements of user– item interaction data and item-level credibility la-bels; Amazon datasets, despite their scale, cannot be used as they provide no such credibility anno-tations. We leave the evaluation on larger-scale datasets as future work, and note that our method can be seamlessly deployed on them once such resources become available. 

Ethical Considerations 

This paper aims to develop a credibility-aware gen-erative recommendation model to serve societal good by mitigating the dissemination of uncredi-ble content (e.g., fake news and misinformation) through recommender systems (RSs). All datasets used in our experiments are derived from publicly available sources and contain no personally identi-fiable information.

A Related Work 

A.1 Generative Recommendation 

Existing GR methods (Hou et al., 2025c; Rajput et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2026d) generally follow a two-stage design: (i) tokenization stage that repre-sents each item with discrete tokens, and (ii) gen-eration stage that generates a sequence of tokens to represent the recommended items (Rajput et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2026; Hou et al., 2026; Ding et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2025). 

Item Tokenization. From the perspective of tok-enization, existing methods can be broadly catego-rized into clustering-based and vector quantization-based approaches. Clustering-based methods orga-nize items into hierarchical groups and represent each item with the cluster ID (Si et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024d; Lv et al., 2024). Vector quantization-based methods learn a shared codebook of discrete tokens and represent each item with the correspond-ing tokens in the codebook (Wang et al., 2025b; Mei et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Hou et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025a; Zheng et al., 2025). For example, TIGER (Rajput et al., 2023) pioneered this direction by utilizing RQ-VAE to quantize item content embeddings into hierarchi-cal semantic IDs. Subsequent work has sought to enrich the tokenization process: Some works (Wang et al., 2024c; Liu et al., 2025d; Wang et al., 2025c; Ye et al., 2025) integrates collaborative sig-nals from pre-trained recommendation models into vector quantization through contrastive alignment, and LC-Rec (Zheng et al., 2024) aligns a large language model with semantic IDs via multi-task learning to improve the model’s understanding of discrete tokens. SETRec (Lin et al., 2025) learns order-agnostic identifiers to improve the perfor-mance of GR through LLMs. Nevertheless, these methods follow a two-stage paradigm where the tokenizer and the downstream recommender are optimized separately, leading to objective misalignment between the tokenizer and generator. To bridge this gap, recent efforts have explored end-to-end joint optimization: ETE-GRec (Liu et al., 2025b) proposes alternating opti-mization between the tokenizer and recommender with alignment losses, while DIGER (Fu et al., 2026) introduces differentiable semantic IDs via Gumbel-Softmax to enable direct gradient flow from the recommender back to the tokenizer. 

Generation Paradigm. From the perspective of generation, existing GR models can be catego-rized into autoregressive (AR) models and non-autoregressive (NAR) models. Most existing meth-ods adopt AR generation, which generate tokens sequentially from left to right (Rajput et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024c; Fang et al., 2026; Qiao et al., 2026). At industrial scale, HSTU (Zhai et al., 2024) introduces hierarchical sequential transducers with significant scalability, and OneRec (Deng et al., 2025) unifies retrieval and ranking within a sin-gle generative model with preference alignment. However, the AR paradigm suffers from two in-herent limitations: (i) the sequential decoding pro-cess is time-consuming, as each token must wait for all preceding tokens to be generated, and (ii) errors made at earlier generation steps propagate irreversibly to subsequent tokens, resulting in error accumulation (Shi et al., 2025). To address these limitations, NAR models have been proposed (Hou et al., 2025a; Shah et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026b; Mu et al., 2026). Inspired by dis-crete diffusion language models (Nie et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a) and diffusion mod-els in RSs (Lin et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a; Cai et al., 2026c), LLaDA-Rec (Shi et al., 2025) in-troduces a parallel generation paradigm, where all tokens are generated simultaneously conditioned on bidirectional context. This design enables richer inter-token dependency modeling by attending to both preceding and following tokens. Although GR has shown promising results, exist-ing methods generally focus on improving recom-mendation accuracy while ignoring the credibility of the recommended content. To fill this gap, in this paper, we propose a novel GR model that ex-plicitly incorporates content credibility into both the tokenization and generation stages. 

A.2 Credible Content Recommendation 

RSs play a crucial role in helping users quickly lo-cate content they are interested in (Cai et al., 2022, 2023b,a; Bing et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2020, 2022a). However, RSs have been widely recognized as significant contributors to the spreading of uncredible content, including fake news, rumor, and misinformation, across modern web platforms (Fernandez and Bel-logín, 2020; Samuel-Azran and Hayat, 2019). The majority of prior work in this area has centered on two directions: examining whether standard recom-mendation algorithms amplify uncredible content, and developing mitigation strategies. 

Empirical Analysis. The majority of prior work has centered on examining whether standard recom-mendation algorithms amplify uncredible content (Fernández et al., 2021; Pathak et al., 2023; Hornig et al., 2024; Pathak and Spezzano, 2024; Pathak, 2024; Fernandez and Bellogín, 2020). For instance, Pathak et al. (Pathak et al., 2023) conduct a system-atic evaluation showing that collaborative filtering algorithms disproportionately recommend misin-formation to users who have previously engaged with such content, creating self-reinforcing filter bubbles. While these studies provide valuable di-agnostic insights, they remain at the analysis level and do not propose concrete algorithmic solutions. 

Mitigation Strategies. In contrast, a smaller body of work moves beyond analysis to develop novel mitigation strategies (Wang et al., 2022c, 2024a; Sallami et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2026b). Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2022c, 2024a) propose veracity-aware news recommenda-tion frameworks that explicitly disentangle items’ veracity embeddings from event embeddings and solely utilize event embeddings to reduce the ex-posure of fake news. Sallami et al. (Sallami et al., 2023) introduce FANAR, a trust-based adaptation of collaborative filtering that excludes untrustwor-thy neighbors from the recommendation process. More recently, Cai et al. (Cai et al., 2026a) ex-plores to steer diffusion models toward credible content recommendation through two designs: a disentangled diffusion model and the progressively enhancement of credible subspace projection. Although these mitigation methods have demon-strated effectiveness, they are fundamentally built upon discriminative modeling paradigms that rely on computing embedding similarities between user and item embeddings to make recommendations. As such, they are inherently incompatible with the emerging GR paradigm, which formulates the recommendation task as a sequence generation problem over discrete tokens rather than a match-ing problem over continuous embeddings. To bridge this gap, in this paper, we propose a novel credibility-aware GR model.

We compare our proposed CreGR with baselines from three categories:

• GRU4Rec (Hidasi et al., 2015): A session-based recommendation model that employs Gated Re-current Units (GRUs) to model sequential user interactions. • SASRec (Kang and McAuley, 2018): A self-attentive sequential recommendation model that leverages the Transformer’s self-attention mecha-nism to capture long-range dependencies in user interaction histories. • DreamRec (Yang et al., 2023b): A diffusion rec-ommender that generates the target item embed-dings by applying a continuous diffusion process in the embedding space. • PreferDiff (Liu et al., 2025c): A preference-guided diffusion model for sequential recommen-dation that incorporates user preference signals into the diffusion process. 

Credible Recommendation Models 

• Rec4Mit (Wang et al., 2022c): A veracity-aware and event-driven personalized news recommen-dation model designed to mitigate fake news in recommender systems. • HDInt (Wang et al., 2024a): A hierarchical and disentangling interest learning framework for un-biased and true news recommendation. • PRISM (Ma et al., 2025): A diffusion model that bridges user interests and truthfulness for miti-gating fake news exposure in recommendations. • Disco (Cai et al., 2026a): A disentangled dif-fusion model that steers the diffusion process towards credible content recommendation. 

Generative Recommendation Models 

• TIGER (Rajput et al., 2023): A generative re-trieval model for recommender systems that rep-resents each item with semantic IDs generated by RQ-VAE. It generates all tokens of the target item in parallel through a discrete diffusion process.

To intuitively illustrate the effectiveness of CreGR ,we present a case study from the GossipCop dataset in Figure 4. In case 1, the user has historically interacted with four fake news articles, covering topics such as celebrity relationships (e.g., “Fer-gie separated from Josh Duhamel”, “Chris Pratt files for divorce from Anna Faris”) and celebrity family affairs (e.g., “Kylie Jenner admits jealousy over Kendall”, “Katherine Jackson bowing out of guardian biz”). Despite all historical interac-tions being fake news, our model successfully cap-tures the user’s underlying topical preferences — celebrity gossip, particularly regarding relationship changes and family dynamics — while steering the recommendations entirely toward credible content. Specifically, the top-ranked recommendation (Item 3631) is a true news article about “Chris Pratt and Anna Faris are officially divorced”, which precisely matches the user’s interest in the same celebrity couple but provides verified factual reporting in-stead of fabricated claims. This case demonstrates that 

CreGR effectively mitigate uncredible content while still preserving users’ preference-related informa-tion for improving recommendation accuracy. 

G Complexity Analysis 

Compared to standard RQ-VAE quantization and discrete diffusion training, the additional compu-tational overhead introduced by CreGR stems from two components: credibility regularization and the soft weighting strategy. For credibility regulariza-tion, the complexity of computing the distance be-tween each item’s latent representations and the centroids of credible and uncredible clusters is 

O(|I| · d), where |I| is the number of items and d

is the codeword embedding dimension. For the soft weighting strategy, the complexity of identifying uncredible last tokens and computing wi consists of two terms: O(( |S H| + |vn|) · L) for scanning token sequences, where SH and vn denote the to-ken sequences of a user’s historical interactions and the target item respectively, and L is the codebook depth; and O(|I unc |· d) for computing weights over uncredible items, where |I unc | is the number of un-credible items. All additional terms scale linearly with the input size and are negligible relative to the dominant cost of standard RQ-VAE and discrete diffusion training, confirming that CreGR maintains high computational efficiency. 

Computational efficiency of centroid-based cred-ibility regularization. A key design advantage of our credibility regularization (Eq. 11) is its compu-tational efficiency. By computing distances to the centroids of credible and uncredible item clusters rather than to every individual item, we reduce the complexity of the credibility loss from O(|I| 2 · d)

(pairwise) to O(|I| · d) (centroid-based).
