# Rethinking Semantic ID Construction for Generative Recommendation: SimHash with Parallel Decoding and Semantic Alignment

Abstract 

Semantic ID-based generative recommendation represents each item as a sequence of discrete tokens, enabling structured modeling of item semantics. A critical challenge is constructing semantic IDs that are both semantically expressive and computationally efficient. While recent approaches favor complex learned quan-tization, simple hashing-based methods such as SimHash are widely regarded as fundamentally inferior. In this work, we challenge this consensus by showing that the apparent performance gap does not stem from inherent limitations of hash-ing, but rather from a structural mismatch with autoregressive decoding, coupled with the inevitable information loss during rigid discretization. Based on this insight, we propose FLASH, a two-stage framework that revitalizes training-free SimHash tokenization through parallel decoding and explicit semantic alignment. Despite its simplicity, FLASH achieves state-of-the-art performance across mul-tiple datasets without requiring any tokenizer training, while exhibiting stronger generalization in cold-start scenarios. Notably, we demonstrate that semantic alignment acts as a universally effective mechanism across diverse paradigms. Our findings suggest that, with compatible decoding and semantic grounding, simple and efficient tokenizers can achieve performance comparable to complex learned counterparts in generative recommendation. 1 Introduction 

Generative recommendation has emerged as a promising paradigm that frames next-item prediction as a sequence generation problem [25, 45, 43], moving beyond traditional approaches that rely on learned atomic item embeddings and inner product-based retrieval [23, 39, 37, 7]. A key direction within this paradigm is semantic ID (SID)-based recommendation, where each item is represented as a sequence of discrete tokens derived from its content, and a sequence model is trained to generate the semantic ID of the next item [18, 47, 36, 17, 42]. This shift to composable semantic codes enables knowledge sharing across items, improves generalization to rare or unseen items, and eliminates the need for large item-specific embedding tables. A central question is how to construct semantic IDs that are both expressive and well aligned with the decoding process. Among existing approaches, residual quantization (RQ) [41, 24] and product quantization (PQ) [21] are two dominant strategies for semantic ID construction. RQ-based methods, represented by TIGER [36], adopt a Residual-Quantized Variational Autoencoder (RQ-VAE) [24] to produce hierar-chically dependent codes and rely on autoregressive (AR) decoding. However, they require training a dedicated quantizer and are typically limited to short semantic ID sequences (e.g., 4 tokens per

In contrast, PQ-based methods, represented by RPG [17], apply optimized product quantization (OPQ) [11] to generate unordered codes and adopt parallel decoding, thereby improving efficiency and enabling longer semantic IDs. Despite these differences, both approaches depend on learned quantizers, motivating the exploration of training-free alternatives such as Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) [20, 19], which maps item embeddings to discrete codes via random projections. While training-free and efficient, this crude discretization inevitably discards fine-grained semantic information compared to optimized quantizers. In this work, we revisit this con-clusion and show that the perfor-mance gap can be attributed to a mis-match between tokenization structure and decoding paradigm, rather than the tokenization itself. Specifically, SimHash produces codes that do not admit a natural ordering and lack in-herent dependencies across positions, whereas autoregressive decoding im-poses sequential dependencies. Our preliminary study (Figure 1; see Ap-pendix A.3 for detailed setup) shows that replacing autoregressive decoding with parallel decoding significantly improves the performance of LSH-based tokenization, indicating that such incompatibility introduces harmful inductive bias. However, this improvement alone does not fully close the gap with learned quantization methods, suggesting that additional factors beyond decoding compatibility play an important role. Building on this insight, we derive two design principles for effective semantic ID-based generative recommendation: (1) structural compatibility , which requires the decoding paradigm to align with the dependency structure induced by the tokenization method. Crucially, enforcing this compatibil-ity via parallel decoding inherently removes the inference bottleneck and ID length constraint of autoregressive generation, enabling the use of substantially longer semantic ID sequences to boost representational capacity. (2) semantic grounding , which explicitly compensates for the loss of fine-grained information introduced by discrete tokenization, particularly for training-free methods. Guided by these principles, we propose FLASH (Fast LLM-Aligned Sim Hash for Generative Recommendation), a simple yet effective two-stage framework that integrates training-free SimHash tokenization with parallel decoding and LLM-based semantic alignment. In the first stage, items are mapped to semantic IDs using SimHash over pre-computed LLM text embeddings, requiring no tokenizer training. In the second stage, a sequence model encodes user interaction histories. To ensure structural compatibility with SimHash codes, it predicts the semantic ID of the target item using parallel decoding under a multi-token prediction (MTP) objective. To compensate for the semantic information loss introduced by hashing, we incorporate an alignment loss that anchors the learned representations to the original LLM embedding space during training. Overall, our results demonstrate that effective semantic ID-based generative recommendation depends on both structural compatibility and semantic grounding, and that LSH-based methods can perform strongly with appropriate decoding and semantic supervision. Beyond this core insight, FLASH combines competitive recommendation performance with high efficiency through training-free tokenization, parallel inference, and support for long semantic IDs. Furthermore, FLASH exhibits strong generalization in cold-start settings. SimHash-based semantic IDs exhibit structural properties that inform model design. First, codes across positions are generated individually without residual dependencies. Moreover, SimHash imposes no intrinsic ordering over positions, making autoregressive decoding structurally mismatched. Finally, the discretization process introduces information loss despite preserving coarse-grained similarity. These properties motivate the design of FLASH in §2.2, where parallel decoding aligns with unordered structure and semantic alignment compensates for information loss.

2.2.3 Semantic Alignment for Information Loss

3.2 Overall Performance 

We compare FLASH with three categories of baselines across four public datasets and report the overall performance in Table 1. FLASH consistently achieves the best performance on all datasets and metrics, with relative improvements on N@5 ranging from 5.51% to 14 .46% , demonstrating its effectiveness and robustness. Compared to item ID-based methods, FLASH achieves substantial improvements across all datasets, suggesting that representations grounded in item semantic information provide more informative signals than discrete item identifiers for recommendation. Within the semantic ID-based paradigm, FLASH outperforms both RQ-based and PQ-based methods. For example, compared to RPG, the strongest PQ-based baseline, FLASH achieves gains of 5.18% on R@10 and 11 .02% on N@10 on the Toys dataset. This result is particularly notable given that FLASH employs a training-free tokenization scheme, in contrast to learned quantization approaches that require additional training objectives and model parameters. FLASH is also more efficient than TIGER due to its training-free tokenization and parallel prediction (see Figure 3 and Appendix B.1). The superior performance of FLASH can be attributed to three key design choices. First, SimHash enables efficient semantic ID construction while preserving coarse-grained semantic similarity from LLM-based embeddings. Second, parallel decoding aligns naturally with the unordered structure of LSH-based tokens, avoiding the mismatch introduced by autoregressive decoding. Third, the semantic alignment objective compensates for information loss introduced by discrete hashing, retaining fine-grained semantic signals. 3.3 Ablation Study 3.3.1 Analysis of Structural Compatibility 

To investigate the impact of structural compatibility, we replace SimHash with alternative tokenization methods while keeping the downstream sequence modeling pipeline fixed, following the dataset-specific configuration of FLASH reported in Table 4. Specifically, we consider Random code assignment with no semantic basis and three learned quantizers: RQ-VAE , RQ-KMeans , and OPQ .The results are reported in Table 2. SimHash achieves competitive or superior performance compared to learned quantization methods across most datasets, despite requiring no tokenizer training. Random assignment performs sub-stantially worse, with over 40% lower N@10 than SimHash on average, showing that structural compatibility alone is insufficient without meaningful semantic information. Among learned tok-enizers, OPQ generally outperforms the residual methods under the same downstream modeling pipeline, further suggesting the importance of compatibility between the tokenization structure and the decoding paradigm. However, the relatively small performance gaps among learned tokenizers and SimHash indicate that parallel decoding and semantic grounding can partially mitigate differences in tokenization. Notably, SimHash substantially reduces tokenization cost, requiring less than 0.5

seconds on CPU across all datasets, compared with tens to hundreds of seconds for learned quantizers. To further characterize the tokenizers beyond recommendation performance, we compare the code collision behavior of SimHash and RQ-VAE across multiple datasets and codebook configurations. As shown in Figure 3, the number of collisions for SimHash is consistently low across all datasets and decreases as the number of codebooks increases. For example, on the Beauty dataset, collisions reduce from 78 at m = 4 to only 1 at m = 64 . A similar trend is observed across larger datasets such as CDs, where collisions remain a small fraction of the total number of items even at scale. Importantly, the collision count for SimHash is comparable to, and in some cases lower than, that of RQ-VAE under the same configurations. 3.5 Cold-Start Generalization 

To evaluate the generalization ability of FLASH, we adopt a cold-start setting following TIGER [36], where a subset of target items is held out from training and used only for evaluation. Full details are provided in Appendix A.4. From Figure 6, we can observe that FLASH consistently outperforms all baselines across all datasets. Compared to SASRec, the performance gap highlights the advantage of semantic ID-based modeling over conventional item ID-based methods in handling unseen items. Compared to TIGER, which employs learned quantization, FLASH still achieves better performance. This improvement can be attributed to two key factors. First, the training-free SimHash tokenization preserves coarse-grained semantic similarity derived from LLM-based text embeddings, enabling stable and meaningful representations for unseen items without relying on interaction data or learned codebooks. Second, the semantic alignment objective further anchors item representations to the original embedding space, improving generalization under distribution shift. Overall, the superior performance of FLASH demonstrates its ability to generalize to unseen items and highlights the importance of semantic grounding for robust recommendation in cold-start scenarios. 

4 Related Work 

LLM-based Generative Recommendation. Traditional sequential recommendation relies on item ID-based paradigms, where each item is represented as a unique identifier and predicted via embed-ding matching [23, 15, 39, 40, 32, 14, 6, 3, 27, 5]. Although effective, such approaches lack semantic compositionality and struggle to generalize to new or infrequent items. With the advancement of large language models (LLMs), generative recommendation has emerged as a promising paradigm that for-9mulates recommendation as sequence generation [45, 13, 26, 29, 30, 22]. Some methods [12, 46, 1] treat recommendation as a text generation task, leveraging pretrained semantic knowledge. However, operating directly in natural-language space without explicit discrete item representations makes efficient large-scale retrieval challenging. Semantic ID-based methods address this limitation by representing items as discrete token sequences from a shared vocabulary [36, 17, 9], enabling com-positional representations and scalable retrieval. Despite their success, existing approaches differ significantly in how semantic IDs are constructed and decoded. 

Semantic ID-based Generative Recommendation. Existing approaches to semantic ID construction can be broadly categorized into two dominant quantization-based strategies. RQ-based methods [36, 42, 10, 28] produce hierarchically dependent codes typically paired with autoregressive decoding. For example, TIGER [36] employs RQ-VAE, which iteratively quantizes item embedding residuals across multiple codebooks to construct a multi-level tuple of discrete codewords as the item’s semantic ID. In contrast, PQ-based methods [17, 16, 35] generate unordered codes compatible with parallel prediction. Despite these differences, both approaches rely on data-dependent quantizers learned from item embeddings, introducing additional training complexity and potential misalignment with recommendation objectives. Recent work further explores semantic IDs tailored to bidirectional or diffusion-based decoding [38, 31, 34]; see Appendix C.1 for discussion. As an alternative to learned quantization, locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) methods [20, 19, 44], such as SimHash [4], provide a training-free mechanism for constructing semantic IDs via random projections, without requiring quantizer learning. However, prior work [36] reports inferior performance of hashing-based tokenization, leading to the common belief that such methods are less effective. In this work, we revisit this conclusion by examining the interaction between tokenization structure and decoding strategy in generative recommendation. 

5 Conclusion 

In this work, we demonstrate that the effectiveness of semantic ID-based generative recommendation depends less on tokenization complexity and more on structural compatibility with the decoding paradigm and alignment with the underlying semantic space. Specifically, our empirical results reveal that a lightweight, training-free SimHash tokenization scheme can achieve competitive performance when paired with parallel decoding and explicit semantic grounding. It also exhibits stronger generalization than multi-stage learned quantization pipelines. These findings suggest a critical shift in perspective for generative recommendation: cross-stage compatibility and semantic alignment may be more important than increasing tokenization complexity. This highlights the potential of simple, principled designs to rival or even outperform more complex alternatives. 

Limitations. Despite its strong performance and efficiency, FLASH has several limitations. First, it relies on pre-computed text embeddings, so its effectiveness depends on the quality of the underlying representations and may degrade when textual information is sparse or noisy. Second, hashing-based tokenization may introduce collisions. Although our empirical analysis shows that collisions are limited under the evaluated configurations, they may become more pronounced at larger scales or under different codebook settings, potentially reducing the discriminability of semantic IDs. Third, our experiments are conducted on public recommendation benchmarks, and industrial-scale validation remains necessary to assess the scalability and robustness of FLASH in real-world recommendation systems. Finally, our study focuses on textual signals and does not explicitly incorporate other modalities such as images. Extending the framework to richer multimodal settings is an important direction for future work, particularly when item semantics cannot be fully captured by text alone.

A.2 Baselines 

We compare FLASH against three categories of baselines: 

(1) Item ID-based methods. These methods represent each item using a unique identifier and learn item embeddings solely from interaction data. Caser [40] models user–item interaction sequences using convolutional neural networks to capture both union-level and point-level sequential patterns. GRU4Rec [15] utilizes recurrent neural networks to model sequential dependencies in user behavior. HGN [32] introduces a hierarchical gating mechanism to adaptively combine long-term user preferences with short-term sequential signals. BERT4Rec [39] applies bidirectional Transformer encoders with a masked item prediction objective to capture contextual dependencies in interaction sequences. SASRec [23] employs a self-attention mechanism to model user sequences, enabling efficient capture of long-range dependencies with a unidirectional Transformer. S3-Rec [48] enhances a SASRec-style Transformer with self-supervised pretraining objectives. 

(2) RQ-based semantic ID methods. These methods construct semantic IDs via residual quan-tization and typically rely on autoregressive decoding. TIGER [36] adopts an RQ-VAE [24] to produce hierarchical semantic IDs and models them with autoregressive decoding, generating tokens sequentially with beam search. LETTER [42] learns semantic IDs via residual quantization with additional semantic, collaborative, and diversity constraints to improve token quality. DIGER [10] introduces differentiable residual quantization to jointly optimize tokenization and recommendation in an end-to-end framework. 

(3) PQ-based semantic ID methods. These methods derive semantic IDs using product quantization, producing independent codes that enable flexible modeling. RecJPQ [35] applies joint product quantization to represent items as combinations of shared sub-embeddings. VQ-Rec [16] learns vector-quantized item representations via codebooks and integrates them into sequential recommenda-tion models. RPG [17] generates long semantic IDs via optimized product quantization (OPQ) [11] and models them with parallel decoding to enable efficient and scalable inference.

For all experiments, we use OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large 1 model to extract semantic embeddings for each item. These embeddings are used as inputs to different quantization methods (e.g., RQ-VAE, OPQ, LSH) to ensure a unified input representation, where SimHash is adopted as the specific LSH instantiation.

A.4 Cold-Start Evaluation Details 

To construct the cold-start setting, we follow TIGER’s evaluation protocol [36]. We then remove all interactions involving these items from the training set, ensuring that they remain completely unseen during training. Finally, we evaluate model performance on the subset of test sequences whose target items belong to this set, thereby simulating a strict cold-start scenario. For item ID-based baselines such as SASRec [23], embeddings of unseen items are randomly initialized at evaluation time, as they are not updated during training.

B.3 Qualitative Analysis of Code Embeddings 

We visualize the learned code embeddings on the unit sphere before and after training across different datasets. As shown in Figure 9, the embeddings remain broadly distributed on the unit sphere after training, while exhibiting more structured patterns. This indicates that training organizes the representation space while preserving its global geometry. These observations qualitatively support the role of semantic grounding. While SimHash provides an initial coarse-grained semantic structure through token assignments, the alignment objective further refines the embedding space, enabling the model to capture more meaningful semantic relationships. 

B.4 Hyperparameter Analysis on Additional Datasets 

We report additional hyperparameter analysis results on the remaining datasets in Figure 10, showing that the overall trends are consistent across datasets. Specifically, moderate values of the alignment weight λalign consistently yield the best performance, whereas overly large values degrade perfor-mance. Similarly, increasing the number of codebooks improves performance up to a certain point, after which the gains saturate or slightly decline. For the codebook size, larger values provide modest improvements initially but exhibit diminishing returns as the size increases. These results further validate that the effects of key hyperparameters are stable across datasets and support the conclusions drawn in the main text.
