Abstract —Lifecycle overhead silently consumes accelerator capacity across large-scale recommendation training fleets. Our largest recommendation workloads process tens of billions train-ing examples per day on thousands of GPUs. Before this work, only 50-60% of their end-to-end wall time advanced training on new data. We present a fleet-scale study of this lifecycle overhead and a set of optimizations spanning the full training stack. We use Effective Training Time (ETT%) as an operational framework to instrument lost time, localize it to independently owned infrastructure components, and expose work repeated across job restarts. This analysis guides optimizations like communication elimination and pipeline overlap during trainer initialization; dynamic-shape handling, autotuning pruning, and reusable Py-Torch 2 compilation caches; asynchronous checkpointing; stan-dalone model publishing; and reductions in recovery cost. We evaluate the optimizations on representative models and measure their impacts in our training fleet. ETT% improves on every benchmark, by 15.5% on average, and reaches 85% on our largest workload. Fleet-wide ETT% rose from about 80% to above 90% after deployment. 

Index Terms —recommendation systems, training efficiency, effective training time, distributed training, PyTorch, large-scale ML infrastructure 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recommendation quality decays as user behavior drifts, so a production recommendation model is only as good as its freshness: how quickly training turns new interaction data into a deployed model. Hence, a recommender must absorb interaction data continuously while sharing the training fleet with other production workloads [9]. At this scale, training efficiency is not only the speed of a training step. A job occupies GPUs from allocation until release, including periods in which it waits, initializes, compiles, checkpoints, publishes, or recovers. Our largest ads recommendation models use thousands of GPUs and train on tens of billions examples per day. Our largest ads recommendation models only spent 50-60% of its end-to-end wall time training on new data. At this scale, time spent outside the training loop is idle GPU capacity, not just a minor startup cost. Existing metrics do not close this gap. Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) measures efficiency inside the training loop [2], [4], [8], but it does not account for the rest of the job lifecycle. ML Productivity Goodput provides the correct end-to-end view by separating productive work from idle and lost work [12], [16]. In our environment, however, its top-level categories do not identify the individual infrastructure phase that regressed or the team that can correct it. Optimization requires both an end-to-end objective and a diagnostic path from that objective to an owned system component. We address this operational gap with Effective Training Time (ETT%), the fraction of end-to-end wall time spent advancing training on new data. The central idea is the hierarchy behind the scalar. At Level 1, ETT% attributes lost time to Time to Start (TTS), Time to Recover (TTR), and Number of Failures (NoF). At Level 2, instrumented phase boundaries localize the loss to six components, each mapped to an infrastructure system and an owning team. This structure turns a fleet-level efficiency regression into a bounded systems problem with a measurable target. The individual optimization techniques in this study are established systems methods, including asynchronous check-pointing and fast recovery [7], [15]. Our contribution is the fleet-scale process for deciding which method to apply, measuring its end-to-end return, and preserving the gain as models and infrastructure evolve. The hierarchy also exposes interactions across the lifecycle: when recovery repeats part of startup, an improvement to that shared phase reduces both TTS and TTR. We measure this restart effect rather than assuming that the benefits of separate changes are independent. We evaluate the framework on 7 recommendation models running on NVIDIA H100 clusters from 8 to 2,000 GPUs, and we track the deployed result across hundreds of models arXiv:2610.02057v1 [cs.IR] 1 Oct 2026 Fig. 1. Large-scale recommendation and ranking models train con-tinuously on fresh data from user interactions [6], [9]. Unlike an LLM pre-training run over a largely static corpus [3], recommendation training uses shorter jobs and restarts more often. A model refresh on a new data window, a hyperpa-rameter change during tuning, an infrastructure failure, or a preemption can each force a restart. Each restart repeats initialization work, including process group creation, model instantiation, data pipeline warm-up, and PT2 compilation. 

A. A Measured Time Breakdown 

We instrumented 7 recommendation models on NVIDIA H100 GPUs and recorded a timestamp at each phase boundary. Figure 1 shows one representative job on 128 GPUs before any optimization in this paper. Effective training takes 90.0 of the 151.5 minutes, or approximately 60%. PT2 compilation takes 30.0 minutes (20%), trainer initialization takes 15.0 minutes (10%), scheduling takes 6.0 minutes (4%) with wasted training time (5%) and shutdown (1%) taking a combined 9.1 minutes. Together, these non-training phases account for the remain-ing 40% of wall time. Moreover, this lifecycle overhead is not necessarily paid only once: each restart can repeat the cold-start phases. The cost of a restart follows from two quantities. Let C

be the cold-start time: scheduling, hardware setup, trainer initialization, and compilation. Let F be the number of restarts in a day. One job then spends at most (1 + F ) C minutes per day outside the training loop. With C = 51 minutes and F = 3 , this amounts to 204 minutes of allocated GPU time that does not advance training. On a 1,000-GPU allocation, this is approximately 3,400 GPU-hours per day. A warm restart can skip some phases, so this figure is an upper bound. 

B. Why Existing Metrics Do Not Localize the Problem 

Two established metrics measure training efficiency. They differ from ETT% in scope, output, and operational ownership. 

MFU does not cover time outside the training loop. 

Model FLOPs Utilization [2] covers the training steps only. It is the ratio of the model FLOPs per second that a job completes to the peak FLOPs per second of the hardware. MFU is undefined outside the training loop. A job can therefore sustain 95% MFU on every step and still spend only 60% of its wall time on new data. For a three-week LLM run, the cold start amortizes to 0.2%, so MFU can be a good proxy for end-to-end efficiency. For a 151.5-minute recommendation job, the cold start alone is 34%. We call this divergence the 

lifecycle efficiency gap .

Goodput covers the lifecycle but does not decompose. 

ML Productivity Goodput [12], [16] covers the full job lifetime and separates lost computation from idle time. When Goodput decreases, an operator can identify the broad type of loss, but not necessarily the infrastructure phase that caused it. A compiler regression, a scheduling policy change, and a storage slowdown can all move the top-level metric in the same direction. This granularity does not match our team structure. Separate teams own the compiler, scheduler, storage layer, and trainer framework. No single team can diagnose or improve the aggregate metric independently, so remediation begins with an investigation across team boundaries. ETT% retains the lifecycle scope of Goodput and adds the phase granularity needed in our environment. It decomposes into Level-1 and Level-2 sub-metrics, and each Level-2 com-ponent maps to one infrastructure system and one owning team (Section III). 

C. Why Recommendation Workloads Differ 

Three structural properties make lifecycle overhead a first-order cost for recommendation training compared to LLM pre-training. 

1) Short jobs amplify the cold start. Cold-start cost is approximately fixed per restart and does not scale with job duration. A 51-minute cold start is 0.2% of a three-week LLM run and 34% of a 151.5-minute recommendation job. A shorter job pays a larger share. 

2) Heterogeneous architectures increase compilation time. A recommendation model combines embedding tables with billions of parameters, multi-layer perceptrons, multi-head attention, and feature-interaction modules [9]. These modules produce a large computation graph with dynamic tensor shapes. A dynamic shape can trigger recompilation, so the PyTorch 2 compiler needs more than one hour on our largest models from a cold cache [10]. Section IV-B reports the compilation time per model. 

3) Streaming data adds a warm-up phase. LLM training reads batches from a pre-tokenized static dataset. Recom-mendation training connects to a continuously updated data source. Before the first step, it validates schemas, negotiates partitions, and buffers the first batches. This data preprocessing pipeline (DPP) warm-up adds 3 to 5 minutes that a static-dataset workload does not pay. These three properties explain the 40% of wall time that motivate the ETT% framework in Section III and the opti-mizations in Section IV.

A. Definition 

We define Effective Training Time (ETT%) [11] as the fraction of end-to-end wall time spent consuming new training data. Because total wall time varies with model architecture, complexity, and data volume, directly measuring productive time is impractical. We therefore adopt a complementary formulation based on measuring idle time and failure-induced overhead. The formulation reveals that GPU idle time is governed by three complementary factors: Time to Start (TTS), Number of Failures (NoF) and Time to Recover (TTR). Consequently, ETT% degrades whenever initialization overhead grows or failure frequency increases, both of which amplify total idle time relative to training.

As seen in Table I, we decompose ETT% into three Level-1 (L1) sub-metrics. TTS is the one-time cold-start idle time per attempt - scheduling, hardware setup, trainer initialization, and compilation, before the first step that advances the data cursor. TTS and TTR thus quantify per-attempt overhead from a system optimization perspective, while NoF captures interruption frequency from a reliability perspective and sets the cumulative recovery cost as NoF × TTR.

A. Trainer Initialization Optimizations 

Trainer initialization runs seven sub-stages in sequence: device initialization, process group creation, preprocessing creation, training module creation, plugin initialization, pre-training setup, and first-batch retrieval. This phase takes 15.0 minutes on the job in Section II. We report Time to Start (TTS) rather than the phase time us-ing Model D as a representative. Each of the three techniques below moves work off the critical path. It does not make any single sub-stage faster, so a phase-local measurement hides its effect. The baseline TTS is 31.8 minutes on Model D of Figure 6. 

1) Communication Optimizations: Every rank needs the global metadata of each sharded tensor, that is the mapping of its shards to devices. The original path built this metadata with one all_gather collective per embedding table [5]. Each rank contributed its local shard metadata, and every rank then assembled the global view. The number of collectives therefore grew with the number of tables, and a representative model has hundreds of them. This behavior came from open-source Torchrec [13], not from our own trainer code. Every rank already has the sharding plan before this step, and the plan contains the shard layout. A rank can there-fore build its own portion of the global metadata without a collective. We replaced the per-table collectives with local construction from the broadcast plan. The fix landed in the open-source torchrec tree, so all three stacks received it at once. TTS decreases from 31.8 to 30.2 minutes, or 5%. The sharding phase itself decreases from 110 to 24 seconds on 128 GPUs. 

2) Pipeline Optimizations: Several initialization sub-stages have no data dependency on each other, and the original implementation still ran them in sequence. The costly pair is the data preprocessing pipeline (DPP) warm-up and PT2 compilation. DPP warm-up connects to the streaming data sources, validates schemas, and fetches the first training batch, which takes 3 to 5 minutes. PT2 compilation transforms the model code into optimized kernels, at the cost reported in Section II. PT2 compilation needs at least one batch to trace the com-putation graph. That batch came from the DPP. Compilation therefore waited until the DPP delivered it (Figure 2a). The compiler needs a batch with the correct shape and data type. It does not need real training data. We therefore supply a synthetic fast-batch at trainer startup. Fast-batch removes the dependency of PT2 compilation on the DPP, so three branches now run in parallel (Figure 2b):  

> •

the DPP warm-up, which fetches the real first batch in the background,  

> •

PT2 compilation, which starts at once on the fast-batch, and  

> •

train model creation, which depends on neither. The critical path now follows the longest of these three branches instead of their sum. PT2 compilation is the longest branch. TTS decreases from 30.2 to 25.4 minutes. 

3) Parallel Component Initialization: The same argument applies to the remaining independent sub-stages. We start the trainer components and the DPP warm-up together rather than one after the other. TTS decreases from 25.4 to 23.1 minutes. 

4) Combined Effect: The per-technique values above come from the earlier report of this work [11], which enabled the techniques one at a time. The three techniques together decrease TTS from 31.8 to 23.1 minutes, a reduction of 27 percent. The compilation optimizations of Section IV-B decrease it further to 18.6 minutes, for a total TTS reduction of 41%. A restart repeats the phases that TTS covers, so a TTS re-duction also decreases Time to Recover. Section V-C measures this compounding effect across restarts. 

B. PyTorch 2.0 Compilation 

PyTorch 2 (PT2) compilation converts eager model code into optimized graphs and kernels, improving steady-state training throughput at the cost of work before the first pro-ductive step [1]. For large recommendation models, this work can exceed one hour because dynamic tensor shapes trigger additional graphs, user-defined Triton kernels [14] expand the autotuning search, and cache misses force compilation artifacts to be regenerated [10]. The cost affects both Level-1 metrics: it is paid during TTS and can recur during TTR after a failure. We address these costs through three classes of optimiza-tion: fewer compiled graphs, less autotuning search, and reusable artifacts. All three change only how the model compiles, not what it computes. 

Avoiding unnecessary graph recompilation. Recommen-dation batches contain tensors whose dimensions vary with Fig. 2. Trainer initialization pipeline. feature cardinality and input density. Without an explicit dynamic-shape contract, TorchDynamo specializes a graph for an observed shape and can compile another graph when that shape changes. We applied mark_dynamic to dimensions that are expected to vary, allowing one graph to cover a range of inputs. We also prevented Dynamo from tracing into selected built-in neural-network modules when doing so created avoidable graph breaks and guard overhead. 

Reducing autotuning search. Triton autotuning bench-marks multiple kernel configurations before selecting the fastest one [17]. This search became a dominant cost as the number and complexity of user-defined kernels increased. We profiled the kernels that consumed the most autotuning time, selected configurations that preserved runtime performance, and committed those configurations as static defaults. This re-moved repeated searches from the critical path while retaining performance. 

Making compilation artifacts reusable. We found that profile-guided optimization could produce nondeterministic symbolic hashes, changing cache keys for otherwise equivalent graphs. Fixing the hash stabilized cache lookup across attempts and jobs. We then used Mega-Cache [18] to package the compilation state needed by Dynamo, AOTAutograd, Inductor, Triton, and autotuning into one portable archive. A later attempt can restore this archive instead of reconstructing each cache independently. This matters most for TTR, where cache reuse can remove most compilation work from a recovery attempt.

C. Checkpoint Optimization 

A checkpoint generally saves model parameters, optimizer states, and training progress to persistent storage and is essen-tial for fault tolerance [7] but traditionally blocks the training loop, consuming memory bandwidth and causing GPU idle time. Reducing checkpoint save time directly improves ETT% by minimizing training loop interruptions. Moreover, faster saves enable more aggressive checkpoint interval tuning and unlock-ing additional ETT% gains through the interplay between the following three components of wasted training time: 

1) Unsaved Training Time (UTT): upon a job failure, all training progress since the last successful checkpoint is lost. 

2) Checkpoint Save Blocking Time (CSBT): this is the time spent saving checkpoints per-checkpoint that also block the training loop. 3) Number of Checkpoint Save Attempts (NoS): this is defined by the checkpoint save interval where the number of save attempts is inversely proportional to the save interval. These three components exhibit an inherent trade-off gov-erned by the checkpoint interval: more frequent checkpoints reduce unsaved progress upon failure but increase cumulative blocking overhead. By our definition, wasted training time is defined as follows: 

WTT = UTT + NoS × CSBT (2) 

Tuning Checkpoint Frequency: Given a known failure rate, the checkpoint interval can be tuned to minimize this sum. From analysis on jobs, Figure 3 illustrates this trade-off under three scenarios with checkpoint save blocking time = 15s, 30s and 45s per checkpoint and 3 failures per end-to-end training job. As the save interval time increases, WTT% initially decreases and reaches a minima after which it rises gradually as unsaved training loss from job failures then begins to dominate. This convex relationship indicates an optimal checkpoint frequency that balances the cost of frequent saves against the risk of progress lost upon failure.  

> Fig. 3. Checkpoint save interval vs wasted training time

Reducing Checkpoint Save Blocking Time: The following are strategies implemented to cut down on the time saving a checkpoint blocking training progress. As seen in Fig 4, these techniques have lead to a 77% reduction fleet-wide in checkpoint save blocking time which allows us to get back to the productive training loop sooner. 

a) Asynchronous Checkpoint : We implemented async checkpoint, which creates a checkpoint copy in CPU memory, allowing the training loop to resume immediately while a background process completes the checkpoint upload. This decouples checkpoint I/O from the training critical path. 

b) PyTorch Native Staging : Async checkpointing initially used a custom stream staging path: the trainer copied tensors one at a time into a fixed-size shared memory buffer drained concurrently by a separate uploader process, bounding trainer memory by a configured budget rather than by checkpoint size. Because the trainer stalled whenever that buffer filled, save-blocking time was gated by upload throughput. We replaced it with a PyTorch-native staging solution (DCP AsyncStager [19]) that performs a single bulk D2H copy into pinned buffers, taking upload back-pressure off the critical path and reducing save blocking time at the cost of moderately increased trainer host memory.

D. Shutdown and Model Publishing 

Model publishing converts the final training state into an inference-ready snapshot. We originally ran publishing as the last stage of the trainer. This design kept the final model state, its configuration, and the publishing logic in one process. It also provided a simple completion contract: the training job was complete only after it produced a deployable snapshot. This coupling wasted GPU capacity as models and their publish workloads grew. The trainer stopped using the GPUs after the final training step, but the scheduler retained the complete allocation until publishing finished. Snapshot gener-ation does not require accelerator compute, so this design held scarce GPUs while CPU and storage work completed. It also coupled two unequal failure domains. A non-GPU failure in the publish stage could invalidate a completed GPU workflow and force the trainer job to restart. Under the old design, publish-stage failures affected 11–22% of jobs. We separated the stages through a durable checkpoint handoff. The trainer now writes an anchor checkpoint and terminates, which releases its GPU allocation. A separate CPU job reads the checkpoint and produces the inference-ready snapshot. This job can scale and retry independently without repeating training. The change reduced GPU-held shutdown time, a Level-2 component of ETT%, by approximately 30 minutes per job. Figure 5 contrasts the two designs. Sec-tion V-B reports the measured effect in Figure 6: for the models on which standalone publishing was active, shutdown time fell from more than 25 minutes per job to 3.3 minutes or less. E. Scheduling Optimization 

Job scheduling is a first-order lever on Effective Training Time: both overhead terms, TTS and TTR, are set by how and where a job is placed. The main culprit is preemption. Under fleet-wide congestion the scheduler reclaims a running job’s capacity for higher-priority work, killing it mid-flight and forc-ing a restart from the last saved checkpoint. Each preemption increments the failure count and adds a full recovery cycle -re-queueing, re-initialization and re-processing data. Entering ETT as the NoF x TTR term, these grow wall time without growing productive training time. We then worked with scheduling infrastructure teams to reduce this cost:  

> •

Stage-aware preemption: The scheduler has no visibility into a job’s training stage. If the trainer shares this, preemption can favor jobs that just checkpointed, where wasted work is minimal.  

> •

Tenant scheduling knobs: Starting a queued but entitled job immediately requires evicting whoever holds the hardware. These knobs let a tenant admin say ”my jobs can wait up to X minutes for capacity to free up naturally” trading queue latency for fewer preemptions and higher completion rates.  

> •

Crash-loop avoidance: locally-greedy ranker and allo-cator decisions can thrash a job through constant allocate - preempt - requeue cycles which is now prevented by targeted system-level detection. It was non-trivial to attribute these scheduling optimizations to ETT% however, given the non-determinism involved, the large-scale systems this was affecting and a multitude of other extraneous factors in the scheduling sub-system that results in final job allocation. V. E VALUATION 

A. Experimental Setup 

Workloads. We evaluate ETT% on 7 recommendation and ranking models. We selected the models by GPU consump-tion. The set is deliberately heterogeneous. It spans more than two orders of magnitude in GPU count, several model architectures, and different checkpoint policies. We refer to the benchmark models as Model A to Model F. Figure 6 lists the GPU count of each model. The set covers the largest GPU consumers in the fleet and not every recommendation workload. 

Hardware. All jobs run on NVIDIA H100 GPUs in clus-ters. The configurations range from 8 to 2,000 GPUs. The clusters are shared with other traffic, so we do not control queueing delay or cluster load. 

Experimental design. We compare two jobs for each benchmark model. The baseline job uses the configuration from before this work. The optimized job enables the tech-niques of Section IV. Both jobs of a pair use the same model code, the same GPU count and the same checkpoint interval. We run 1 baseline job and 1 optimized job per model. 

Induced failures. Failures arrive at random times, so a short experiment cannot measure TTR from natural failures alone. We therefore fix the failure count. We terminate each benchmark job 2 times, and the job then recovers through the normal path. Both jobs of a pair take the same number of failures. NoF is constant inside a pair, so a difference in TTR comes from the optimizations and not from a difference in failure count. Section IV-E treats the natural failure rate separately. 

Data configuration. Each model reads its tables over a model-specific input date range. The models apply different data processing configurations, so equal date ranges do not mean equal data volumes. We therefore compare each baseline job against its own optimized job. We do not compare absolute phase durations across models. Each job runs for 10–12 hours of end-to-end wall time. The checkpoint interval is 30 or 60 minutes, and it is identical inside a pair. 

Measurement. The training system records a timestamp at every phase boundary of the Level-2 decomposition of Section III: scheduling, hardware setup, trainer initialization, PT2 compilation, productive training, checkpoint, recovery and shutdown. A step counts as productive when it advances the data cursor on new data. Work that a job replays af-ter a recovery does not count. One dashboard per training platform aggregates these timestamps. We compute ETT% and its Level-1 and Level-2 components from them, with the definitions of Section III. 

Limitations. Four limitations apply to the results in this section. First, we run 1 job per configuration, so we report single measurements and not means with confidence intervals. This is a deliberate cost trade-off: a baseline run consumes GPU-hours to reproduce a configuration we already know to be worse, and at 8 to 2,000 GPUs, repeating each pair many times is not affordable. Two aspects of the design compensate. The paired setup holds model code, GPU count, checkpoint interval, and NoF fixed within a pair, so the measured differ-ence is attributable to the optimizations. The longitudinal fleet record (Section V-D) then supplies the statistical mass that a single pair cannot, by tracking ETT% across many models over months. Second, standalone publishing (Section IV-D) was not active on every model at measurement time. The shutdown time of those models is therefore unchanged inside the pair. B. End-to-End ETT% Results 

Per-model ETT%. Figure 6 reports ETT% for every model, before and after the optimizations, with the Level-2 compo-nents that produce it. ETT% improved on all six benchmark models. The baseline values span 59–85% and the optimized values span 80–93% with a mean gain of 15.5 percentage points. 

Variation across models. Model B already ran at 85.2% before this work, while Model F ran at 59.2%. The same optimization program therefore returns a different amount on each model, and the largest bottleneck differs between them. This spread is the empirical case for the decomposition. A single fleet-level number is the same for every model, but where the time was lost, and therefore where the work paid off, is not. The per-component measurement is what tells an engineer where to work; the scalar alone cannot. 

Overhead composition. Lost time in the baseline jobs is 15–41% of end-to-end wall time. Recovery, which is NoF multiplied by TTR, is the largest component in every model, accounting for 43–74% of the lost time. Trainer start accounts for 8–19%. Recovery dominates because a job pays TTS once and pays TTR at every failure. 

Attribution of savings. Figure 6 attributes the recovered minutes to the four components. Recovery contributes 58% of the total saving across the six models. Shutdown contributes 23%, checkpoint 11% and TTS 8%. This is a direct attribution, by the metric in which each minute was saved. Because a recovery repeats the startup phases, part of the recovery saving originates in the startup optimizations; Section V-C gives this cascade attribution in full. 

Restart multiplier. TTR fell by more than TTS on every model with the ratio of the two reductions ranging from 1.8– 8.6. Two effects combine. A recovery repeats hardware setup, trainer initialization and compilation, so it inherits every TTS improvement. A recovery also restores a checkpoint and reads a warm compilation cache, which a cold start cannot. The measurement therefore supports the compounding argument of Section III. 

Shutdown. Standalone publishing removed most of the shutdown time where it was active. Models A, B, C and D spend 25.6 to 54.6 minutes per job in shutdown at baseline, and 3.3 minutes or less after. The shutdown values of Models E and F are unchanged because they did not have standalone publishing enabled. 

C. Interaction and Cascade Analysis 

Cumulative per-technique measurements. Section IV-A reports TTS after each initialization technique, and Sec-tion IV-B reports it after the compilation work. The four reductions sum to 13.2 minutes, the whole 41% of TTS. That sum is exact by construction, because each step measures the change from the step before it. A cumulative sequence therefore depends on the order of enablement and cannot show that the techniques are independent. 

Cascade attribution. Figure 6 credits every recovered minute of TTR to the recovery path, which understates the trainer start work. A recovery repeats hardware setup, trainer initialization and compilation, so it inherits the TTS improve-ments. The inherited part is at most NoF multiplied by the TTS reduction. The shared phases therefore give the bound 

ATTS ≤ (1 + NoF ) ∆ TTS . (3) 

Understated TTS contribution. Figure 6 gives the two reductions for each model, and NoF is 2. The TTS work then accounts for 24.8 percent of the recovered minutes across the six models. The direct attribution assigns it 8 percent. The two figures differ by a factor of three. Per model the bound ranges from 9.1 to 37.8 percent. 

Recovery residual. The remaining 41.3 percent of the recovered minutes belongs to the recovery path alone. A recovery reads a warm compilation cache, which a cold start cannot. This asymmetry is why the ratio of the two reductions is 1.8–8.6 rather than one. 

Fast-batch and compilation interaction. Fast-batch and the compilation work act on the same pair of phases, so they interact. Before fast-batch the critical path contains the data preprocessing warm-up and the compilation in sequence. After fast-batch it contains the longer of the two, so the reduction is 

∆fb = min( DPP , PT2 ). (4) The warm-up takes 3 to 5 minutes, as Section II reports. Fast-batch therefore saves the whole warm-up while compilation stays longer than 5 minutes. The compilation time caps the reduction once compilation becomes shorter than the warm-up. The same overlap explains a gap with Section IV-B. Compila-tion contributes 4.5 minutes to the cumulative sequence, while Section IV-B reports a much larger reduction in compilation time itself. We enabled fast-batch first, so compilation was no longer on the sequential path. Only the exposed part of compilation remains in TTS, and the controlled ablation there also uses a smaller model and fewer GPUs. 

Composition of the effects. The two effects compose differently, and the distinction matters for the claim. Across techniques the reductions are additive, because each technique removes a different segment of the critical path. Across restarts each reduction carries the factor (1+ NoF ) of Equation (3). We therefore describe the result as compounding across restarts. We do not describe it as multiplicative across techniques, because Equation (4) shows that one pair overlaps. 

D. Fleet-Wide Longitudinal Results 

Motivation for a longitudinal record. The paired results of Section V-B show that the optimizations work under controlled conditions. They do not show that the gains survive, where model code, data volume and fleet load all change from week to week. We therefore track ETT% for the whole offline training fleet on a continuous dashboard over a 6-month period. 

Fleet trend. Fleet-wide ETT% for offline training rose from about 80% to above 90% over the window. The fleet metric covers more than 500 models, two orders of magnitude more than the six paired benchmarks of Figure 6. The fleet gain is therefore smaller than the mean gain of 15.5 points in Figure 6. That is because the fleet contains models that received no optimization work in this period. It also contains models that already ran at a high ETT%. 

Regression detection. An internal ETT% dashboard is the control loop that holds the gains. Each Level-2 component carries a service level, and a breach alerts its owner. The Level-1 view placed the loss in the NoF term, and the Level-2 view split it between preemption growth and queue wait. The decomposition also falsified the first hypothesis, which blamed application failures because their absolute daily count rose. Once we normalized attempts per job and excluded preemptions, attempts stayed flat and the application failure rate fell. A fleet-level number alone admits no such test. The owning teams then acted on the attributed components, and ETT% recovered over time. 

Capacity saving. The fleet gain converts directly into released capacity, because a job that spends less wall time outside the training loop returns its GPUs to other workloads sooner. We do not disclose the absolute fleet-compute figure. 

Threats to the fleet measurement. Two effects limit this record. First, a subset of models migrated between training platforms during the window, making their ETT% unstable across the migration. Second, the fleet composition changed over time, so the population at the end of the window differs from the population at the start. The trend therefore measures the fleet as operated, not a fixed cohort. VI. C ONCLUSION 

Training jobs lose a large part of their wall time outside the training loop. We defined Effective Training Time (ETT%) to measure that loss. We decomposed it into components, and each component maps to one infrastructure system and one owning team. We applied the framework to five areas, and implemented core optimizations including trainer initialization optimizations, PyTorch 2.0 compilation time reduction, check-point optimization, shutdown and scheduling optimization. Per-model ETT% rose from 59–85% to 80–93%, and fleet-wide ETT% for offline training rose from about 80% to above 90% in our internal environment. While most prior efforts focus solely on training performance optimization, our study reveals an often overlooked dimension: the analysis and reduction of wasted training time in large-scale systems.
