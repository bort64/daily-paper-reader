ABSTRACT 

Modern online platforms commonly rank ads and organic content separately be-fore blending them into a feed displayed to the user, overlooking externalities: an item’s click-through rate depends on its surrounding content, not only on its own position. Recent learning-based feed generation mechanisms model some of these cross-type interactions to globally optimize for the whole feed’s welfare. However, these approaches either fix the ordering of organic content, or lack exact strategyproofness guarantees for bidders. To combat these shortfalls, we introduce the Maximal-in-Range Transformer (MIRT) mechanism class, which uses a trans-former to generate a range of candidate feeds that jointly order ads and organic content, and selects the welfare-maximizing feed in the range. However, there is a tension: strategyproofness requires the generated range to be bid-independent, even though a candidate feed’s welfare depends linearly on the bids. Our key technical contribution is a reinforcement learning approach that incorporates both candi-date generation and bid-aware selection into training, enabling a bid-independent 

transformer to learn to generate high-welfare ranges by accounting for both in-dividual feed quality and the collective quality of the range. Additionally, we bound the pseudo-dimension of the MIRT class under hard attention, showing that near-optimal expected welfare is learnable with sample complexity polynomial in the transformer size and only logarithmic in the range size. Empirically, MIRT outperforms the previous non-strategyproof state-of-the-art feed models while remaining exactly strategyproof. Our results show that transformer-based auctions can deliver externality-aware whole-feed optimization without sacrificing exact incentive compatibility, removing a major obstacle to their practical deployment. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

In modern feed-based ranking systems, organic content and sponsored ads are commonly ranked in independent pipelines and then blended into a single feed [11; 31; 58]. This architecture is tractable because it treats a post or ad’s click-through-rate (CTR) as primarily a function of its own identity and position relative to other items of the same type. In practice, however, user engagement also depends on the surrounding content. For instance, a hiking post from a close friend may increase the 

click-through rate (CTR) of a nearby outdoor-equipment ad, while several similar ads may instead create fatigue and reduce CTR. This effect is referred to as permutation externalities : the CTR of an ad slot or organic post depends not only on its position but also on the surrounding content. When these externalities are significant, this tractability may result in a toll on total welfare. Motivated by this, we focus on the problem of training transformer models for jointly ranking organic and ad content, taking into account permutation externalities. However, in the advertising context, the goal is not merely to choose a good ranking but also to run an auction. Joint ranking makes it more difficult to enforce incentive compatibility among advertisers because an advertiser may benefit from strategic misreporting to influence the content surrounding its ad. Reusing the aforementioned example of an outdoor equipment advertisement: at a low bid, a 1arXiv:2610.06559v1 [cs.GT] 5 Oct 2026 Preprint. joint feed aggregator might place a hiking post immediately before the ad, generating a high CTR for the advertiser; after the advertiser raises its bid, the aggregator might promote the ad above and far from the hiking post, so the CTR of the ad decreases because it loses the favorable surrounding context despite receiving a higher slot. More formally, a necessary condition for dominant-strategy incentive compatibility (DSIC) , also known as strategyproofness , is that realized advertiser benefits (e.g., CTR times click benefit) is nondecreasing in the advertiser’s bid, but an unconstrained joint ranker need not satisfy this condition. For many mechanism design problems, the techniques of differentiable economics [19] have been used to design state-of-the-art strategyproof mechanisms by training neural networks. For feed generation in particular, recent methods such as CGA [59] and NGA [58] build on this approach. They train transformer models to choose rankings that take into account permutation externalities, and appropriate transfers that minimize incentive compatibility violations. However, these methods can only approximate strategyproofness. In fact, the strategyproofness gap is substantial in practice: in our experiments, we find possible gains from misreporting of more than 10% of advertiser utility (Section 5). To the best of our knowledge, no prior work jointly optimizes the entire ad-organic ordering under permutation externalities while guaranteeing exact DSIC. To directly address incentive issues, we introduce Maximal-in-Range Transformers (MIRT) , a frame-work that combines the expressive power of transformer-based ranking for welfare-maximization under permutation externalities, with a maximal-in-range [37] allocation rule that ensures DSIC. In particular, MIRT uses a transformer to generate a set of candidate ad-organic joint rankings known as the “range”, conditioned on ad and organic content, user features, and other constraints, but independent of advertiser bids. At serving time, a maximal-in-range mechanism observes the bids, selects the candidate among the range that maximizes reported welfare, and charges VCG transfers over the range. Using RL, we train the transformer to generate a small set of feeds that capture contextual interactions among ads and organic content, accounting for both individual feed quality and the collective quality of the range. 

Our Contributions. In Section 2, we present a general model of joint ad-organic optimization. Prior work either considers permutation externalities only among ads, or models ad-organic interactions while taking the organic ordering as fixed input. In Section 3 we introduce the MIRT mechanism class for exactly strategyproof ad-organic joint optimization under permutation externalities. MIRT uses a maximal-in-range approach over a transformer-generated, bid-independent range to ensure strategyproofness. The transformer is trained via RL to generate high-welfare ranges by accounting for both individual feed quality and the collective quality of the range. To our knowledge, MIRT is the first transformer-based approach to feed optimization that provides a strategyproofness guarantee. In Section 4, we provide pseudo-dimension bounds for the welfare class induced by MIRT, and show that the sample complexity of learning a MIRT mechanism with optimal expected welfare within the mechanism class is polynomial in the transformer size and only logarithmic in the range size. Finally, in Section 5 we evaluate MIRT in synthetic environments, comparing against the previous state-of-the-art approaches on welfare, total transfer, and DSIC violations, and studying the effects of range size and externality strength. MIRT attains better welfare than baselines across all tested environments, while also being DSIC by construction. By comparison, the previous SOTA approaches have expected regret under truthful bidding as high as 10% of advertiser utility. 1.1 RELATED WORK 

Differentiable economics. A powerful approach to automated mechanism design, sometimes called differentiable economics , uses deep neural networks to represent and optimize economic mechanisms. The seminal work of Dütting et al. [19] introduces the RegretNet technique, in which an auction is directly represented as a neural network, which is trained to optimize total transfers while minimizing regret violations. There is a wide literature building on this basic idea by improving network architectures and applying the technique to new mechanism design settings [17; 15]. The core limitation of RegretNet is that the guarantee of strategyproofness is only approximate and may be hard to measure. This has motivated the design of specialized network architectures which can train high-performing auctions while ensuring exact strategyproofness by construction, for example by restricting to the class of affine maximizer auctions and learning continuously-parameterized allocation menus [14; 18], by using a more computationally heavy but 2Preprint. more general menu-based approach [48], or by using reinforcement learning to train a sequential mechanism [38; 8]. Several recent works are of particular interest in the context of MIRT. Prior works for feed generation [58; 59] use a RegretNet-inspired training approach (further discussion of these and other works is in Section A), exhibiting large DSIC violations. Duan et al. [18] uses transformer networks to generate the parameters of an affine maximizer (a generalization of MIR mechanisms), but their mechanism design problem is classical additive auction design rather than our feed auctions, their architecture requires a hardcoded range size, and their training techniques search over a continuous space of lottery allocations. 

Machine-Learning Powered Preference Elicitation While differentiable economics uses a learn-ing algorithm to derive a performant mechanism, a related branch of research embeds the learning algorithm within the final auction mechanism itself, using it to aid preference elicitation in com-binatorial settings where the agents’ input space grows exponentially. The foundational works of Blum et al. [6] and Lahaie & Parkes [29] were the first to frame preference elicitation in CAs as a learning problem. More recently, Brero et al. [7; 9] and Weissteiner & Seuken [50]; Weissteiner et al. [52; 51; 53] introduced ML-powered ICAs for auctions in which agents report cardinal bids, and Soumalias et al. [44] extended this approach to auctions driven by demand queries. Soumalias et al. [42] then showed that judiciously combining both query types yields substantially higher efficiency in combinatorial settings. The framework has since been extended to settings without transfers [41], and to mechanisms that incorporate LLMs so that agents can express their preferences in natural language [43]. 

Maximal-in-range mechanisms. The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanisms choose the allocation that maximizes welfare while using transfers that ensure strategyproofness [46; 13; 23]. If welfare maximization is only approximate, then the corresponding VCG transfers are in general not strategyproof. One important exception is the class of maximal-in-range (MIR) mechanisms, which optimize exactly over a restricted range of possible outcomes [30; 37]. As long as the range is chosen without considering bids, the resulting mechanism with VCG transfers is strategyproof. Mechanism design within this class then amounts to designing the range. Some existing theoretical work studies approximately welfare maximizing maximal-in-range mechanisms in single-parameter combinatorial auction settings [21; 25] and in multi-unit auctions [16], as well as sample complexity of learning closely related classes of mechanisms [3; 4].

3 LEARNING MAXIMAL -IN -R ANGE MECHANISMS 

The ideal solution to the feed-generation problem would be to run a VCG advertising auction. A VCG mechanism selects a welfare-maximizing allocation out of all possible allocations and calculates transfers for the corresponding allocation. In our advertisement auction setting, the allocation is an ordered selection of m ads and n organic posts out of a pool of M ads and NO posts, which we call a feed. Instead, we use a maximal-in-range (MIR) mechanism that selects the welfare-maximizing allocation from a smaller set of candidates (range), preserving incentive compatibility as long as the range is generated independently of advertiser bids [37]. An MIR mechanism is defined by a range R of candidate feeds. This range may depend on the context C, including the true ad qualities, organic benefits, item embeddings, and user features, but must be independent of advertiser bids to stay DSIC. Once the range is fixed and bids are observed, the mechanism performs well as long as one element has high performance. Therefore, a good MIR mechanism must predict high quality feeds from the context, and choose a combination of such feeds that covers as well as possible the various bid profiles which may arrive. Our range generation model with parameters θ can produce a context dependent range Rθ (C). Given this range, the mechanism evaluates candidate welfare using the submitted bids, selects the allocation 4Preprint. 

aθ (B, C ), and computes VCG transfers over the same range: aθ (B, C ) ∈ arg max x∈Rθ (C) V (x, B ).

Motivated by the sequential nature of feed construction and the prior works’ models, we propose an RL-trained encoder-decoder architecture to generate a feed by selecting ads and organic posts autoregressively from a candidate pool of items. The model takes item embeddings, organic post benefits, and ad qualities as inputs and generates one feed per rollout. A collection of K distinct feeds creates the candidate range Rθ (C) for the MIR mechanism. 3.1 NEURAL ARCHITECTURE 

We use an encoder-decoder transformer [45] with a pointer output [47; 26] to construct feeds from bid-independent item features (1.38M parameters). The encoder has no positional encoding, therefore it is permutation-equivariant [56] and reordering the candidate pool does not reorder its representations. The pool is encoded once per range and shared across all K rollouts [28]. The decoder then builds a feed autoregressively, at each step applying causal self-attention over the items chosen so far, cross-attending to the encoded pool, and scoring every remaining candidate with a clipped scaled dot-product pointer [5; 26]. Decoder inputs carry a learned positional encoding [20]. Our design has the following considerations: first, a mask at every decoder step blocks items already placed or the items that would violate the feed’s composition, so every rollout is a valid feed by construction rather than by a penalty term. Second, the VCG mechanism needs a diverse menu of feeds both to produce higher welfare and transfer assignments [24; 22]. In our design, each rollout is conditioned on a per-rollout embedding [10; 12] so that the K rollouts of one context produce K

different feeds instead of K copies of similar feeds. 3.2 TRAINING ALGORITHM 

We train the range generator using on-policy policy-gradient reinforcement learning [54]. The policy 

πθ constructs each feed item by item per rollout, conditioned on the context C, repeated K times to generate the range. To score a range, the environment draws D = 8 bid profiles from the prior F and takes the welfare of the best feed in the range under each, so the reward is the expected best-of-K

welfare, r(R; C) = EB∼F [max x∈R V (x, B )] .We follow POMO [28], which builds on REINFORCE policy gradient with an entropy bonus and a baseline shared across rollouts of the same instance rather than a learned critic. For each context, we generate G = 8 ranges for the baseline, force their rollouts to begin with different items so they explore different feeds, and define each range’s advantage as its reward minus the mean reward of the other G − 1 ranges [27], scoring all G on the same D bid profiles. Advantages are standardized and centered across batches. We additionally encourage exploration through forced initial selections and added rollout-diversity vectors. During training, we force distinct initial items for up to min( K, N )

rollouts following Kwon et al. [28] and let the remaining rollouts sample their first item from the policy, including those sampled initial choices. For all training details, including network architecture and optimizer details, see Section E.

MIR baselines. As our main baseline, we consider Random+MIR, which builds a bid-independent range of K = 32 feeds, each drawn uniformly at random from the feasible feeds. MIR then selects the feed with the highest welfare at the reported bids. We also consider a bid-blind greedy method to generate the range (Greedy+MIR). Greedy+MIR scores each item by its bid-independent welfare, meaning the welfare generated by an ad’s quality and a post’s user benefit, and not the bids. It then builds a feed by keeping the highest-scoring feasible set of items and placing the best ones in the most prominent slots. The first feed follows this ranking exactly. The remaining K − 1 feeds perturb the scores with random noise, so the range holds K

distinct high-quality feeds.

6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

We present MIRT, an exactly strategyproof mechanism for whole-feed optimization. MIRT uses an autoregressive transformer to generate the feeds in the range of a maximal-in-range mechanism, optimized via a reinforcement learning signal that takes into account both individual feed and collective range quality. We show that with hard-attention transformers, near-optimal expected welfare is learnable with sample complexity polynomial in the transformer size and only logarithmic in the range width. In extensive experiments, we show that MIRT outperforms prior state-of-the-art approaches in welfare across all settings tested, and remains competitive in total transfer. At the same time, these prior approaches for joint ranking show high strategyproofness violations in our experiments, while MIRT ensures exact strategyproofness by construction. There are many promising directions for future work, including testing improved RL algorithms, extending our methodology to broader classes of mechanisms such as affine maximizers, and applying the same basic approach to many other mechanism design problems beyond feed optimization. 10 Preprint. A RELATED WORK : NEURAL FEED ALLOCATION MECHANISMS 

There are several works using neural networks to learn feed-allocation auction mechanisms, including Ma et al. [35]; Zheng et al. [57]. We highlight two approaches of particular relevance to this work, CGA [59] and NGA [58], both of which consider feed generation in the presence of externalities, and both of which use RL to train their feed generators. Zhu et al. [59] employs an autoregressive decoder that takes context embeddings as input and directly generates ad allocations to maximize expected virtual welfare, taking into account externalities. Their objective is to maximize virtual welfare; the virtual-welfare-maximizing allocation rule has corresponding strategyproof Myerson transfers. However, CGA does not learn the exact optimal allocation rule, and moreover, computing Myerson transfers (via numerical integration) would be infeasibly slow. Therefore, they use a decoupled PaymentNet module to approximate correct transfers, and empirically minimize regret in the fashion of Dütting et al. [19]. The result is an auction that performs well, but for which exact strategyproofness cannot be ensured. Zheng et al. [58] introduces a non-autoregressive feed generator with dual encoders for candidate items and candidate positions, and an evaluator that outputs predicted click-through and conversion rates, along with transfers, which are used to select a final feed. The networks are also trained using an approach similar to Dütting et al. [19] to encourage incentive compatibility. Our approach also uses transformers trained via RL to generate feeds, but we achieve exact strategyproofness and generate our entire range of feeds simultaneously.

D ENVIRONMENT SETUP 

We construct a synthetic environment with large pools of ads and organic posts, whose embeddings are sampled from Gaussian clusters around randomly generated topic centroids. Each user is represented by a weighted average of these centroids, with user-specific weights capturing different interests across topics. Each instance of a dataset is constructed by uniformly sampling M ads and NO organic posts without replacement from their respective pools for a given user embedding. We then sample the ad qualities, bids, and organic benefits for the selected user–item pairs. The resulting item embeddings, ad qualities, and organic benefits form the context C supplied to the range generator. To model user interests, ad qualities, and organic benefits, samples are drawn based on user-item similarities, with their distributions determined by the cosine similarity between the user’s represen-tation and the item’s embedding. Ad qualities and organic post benefits follow lognormal/Weibull distributions characterized by the user-item similarities. However, these distributions and their noise levels are chosen so that the corresponding advertiser and user welfare distributions remain balanced (Appendix Figure 4). Thus, items that are more closely aligned with a user’s interests tend to have higher qualities or benefits, while the two components of welfare stay comparable. E FURTHER MIRT T RAINING DETAILS 

Our MIRT networks have 1.38M parameters, with four encoder and two decoder layers, each with eight attention heads and a 128-dimensional hidden size. We optimize with AdamW [34], using an initial learning rate of 10 −4, weight decay of 10 −4, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. The learning rate follows a cosine decay schedule without restarts [33], over a maximum of 40 epochs with early stopping when there is no more than 0.3 percent improvement in the last 12 epochs. Each optimizer update uses 128 contexts, processed in two micro-batches with gradient accumulation. 

F CGA AND NGA TRAINING DETAILS 

F.1 CONTEXTUAL GENERATIVE AUCTIONS 

CGA [59] is an ad auction mechanism in which each context consists of a user’s features, candidate ad features, and their bids, and predicted click-through rates for that user-ad pair. Each context has 30 candidate ads that are competing for three slots. The architecture consists of three separately trained networks, an evaluator, a generator, and the PaymentNet. 21 Preprint. The evaluator receives a historically shown ad sequence, user features, and existing individual-ad click predictions and is trained on historical click data to predict each ad’s click-through rate. It processes the sequence using a bidirectional LSTM and combines its outputs with user features through an MLP to predict an externality multiplier for each displayed ad. This multiplier is applied to the existing ad-quality estimate to obtain the adjusted click-through rate. Unlike ours, their externality model captures only interactions among ads. The generator uses a self-attention encoder to represent the candidate ads and a GRU decoder to select them one at a time. The frozen evaluator scores the generated sequences, providing rewards for policy-gradient training that aims to maximize the virtual welfare of a three-ad sequence. Finally, PaymentNet receives the selected ads’ representations, bid information, and features derived from allocation probabilities and predicted click-through rates. It predicts a transfer fraction between zero and one, which is multiplied by the advertiser’s bid to determine its transfer per click. PaymentNet is trained to increase total transfer while penalizing ex-post regret, which measures how much advertisers could benefit by misreporting their bids. A tunable hyperparameter controls the strength of this penalty. 

CGA ad-organic implementation. The original CGA paper considers only ads. To create a comparable baseline, we extend its candidate pool and architecture to include organic posts. In our implementation, the evaluator predicts each ad’s click-through rate after accounting for position and surrounding content, and each organic post’s welfare contribution after accounting for position. It is trained using our environment instead of historical data and learns to approximate the externality model used by the welfare function. In the generator, we added separate input projections and learned item-type embeddings to map both ad and organic types into a shared representation space, so the CGA generator’s self-attention is over the entire candidate pool. Finally, similar to the original paper, PaymentNet is trained on synthetic auction contexts with the generator and evaluator frozen. It predicts each displayed advertiser’s transfer per click as a fraction of its bid, to maximize total transfer while penalizing ex-post regret. To estimate regret, we change an advertiser’s bid, regenerate the feed, and recompute transfers and click predictions to measure whether the advertiser benefits from misreporting. Our CGA implementation has approximately 203,000 parameters and uses a two-layer Transformer en-coder with hidden dimension 64, four attention heads, and feed-forward width 256, a 64-dimensional GRU decoder, a bidirectional LSTM evaluator with attention and separate ad and organic prediction heads, and a PaymentNet is a four layer MLP with 13,000 training parameters. F.2 NON -AUTOREGRESSIVE GENERATIVE AUCTIONS 

NGA [58] has a two-network architecture, a generator for allocation, and an evaluator to score allocations and calculate transfers. It extends the CGA architecture to also consider static surrounding organic content in its ad allocation. Unlike CGA’s autoregressive GRU decoder, NGA uses item and position encoders to compute allocation scores for all item-position pairs, then creates candidate feeds item by item through a decoder non-autoregressively. The evaluator scores generator’s feed by producing click-through rates, conversion rates, and transfer fractions per ad. These predictions are used to calculate a score based on the platform’s total transfer for a feed. 

NGA implementation. In our setting, organic posts are selected and reordered alongside ads, so we redesigned NGA’s generator to encode ad-organic context and pass the shared item representations through an MLP to generate a feed. The evaluator is the same simulator-trained network we use for CGA, with separate heads for ad click-through rates and organic welfare contributions. In the welfare experiments, we train the generator with policy gradients on the combined welfare. Transfers come from the separately trained head of the evaluator that predicts transfer fraction to maximize virtual welfare and penalize regret. Our NGA implementation has approximately 180,000 parameters and uses the same encoder, evalua-tor, and a four layer transfer head, replacing the GRU decoder with learned 64-dimensional position embeddings and a three layer MLP that scores all item–position pairs in parallel in the generator. 22 Preprint. F.3 REGRET 

Regret is the largest increase in an advertiser’s utility that misreporting its bid can produce. We estimate it for each advertiser selected under truthful bidding. We iterate over a grid of 20 bids for an advertiser holding the other bids fixed, and recompute the allocation and transfers. We sum the positive gains across advertisers and normalize by the advertisers’ truthful utility. During training, the regret penalty ρ is 1 unless otherwise noted.

G.1 VIRTUAL WELFARE EXPERIMENTS 

Our mechanism design setting is quite challenging, as the combinatorial structure of feeds makes optimization difficult. However, because advertiser welfares are click-through rates multiplied by scalars, it is a single-parameter setting, and therefore relatively well-behaved compared to general multi-parameter mechanism design [2; 36]. In particular, for our setting, assuming that CTR is nondecreasing in a bidder’s bid, and that the lowest bidder type receives 0 utility, the corresponding Myerson transfer is ti(B) = biqi(x(B)) − R bi

0 qi(x(z, B −i)) dz. 

A particularly useful property in single-parameter settings is that it is possible to define a quantity known as virtual welfare. The virtual value of a bidder with value vi is vi − 1−Fi(vi)

fi(vi) . For any DSIC mechanism in which the low type has utility 0, expected virtual welfare equals expected total transfer. Assuming a property known as regularity of the distribution, the virtual-welfare-maximizing mechanism can be combined with an appropriate rule to maximize total transfer, while also being DSIC. For our experiments, we also define a virtual-welfare-optimizing MIR mechanism. Equipped with the appropriate Myerson transfer, this is also DSIC for regular distributions. Results on the default environment are in Figure 6. MIRT competes with other methods on welfare, outperforms baselines on total transfer, and competes with CGA and NGA on total transfer while being exactly strategyproof.
